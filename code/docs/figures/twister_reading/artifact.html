Does TWISTER say anything about the masking-schedule comparison?

Yes, though not about the schedule itself. Species-first order, the only order the schedule comparison needs, has no tilt: it draws teams from the model's own distribution. Random order needs the lookahead, which bends every team by a median 13.6 to 15.2 nats, and the more a team is bent, the more it loses. Drawing exactly from the model's own legal teams (#358) wins back half of random order's win-rate gap to species-first, not the 84% reweighting estimated. The legal Pokémon construction tables remove far more, but that mass was never trained, and it predicts nothing.

1. Scope

The paper never trains anything and never varies a masking schedule. What carries over is its accounting: any sampler that removes probability at each draw and renormalises draws from a different distribution than the model's own, restricted to legal outputs. In our code, the tables of legal Pokémon construction are inside the model (training and every pass), so removing what they forbid is not a tilt. The lookahead and the same-pass legality checks of random order are.

3. The tilt in our teams, and whether correcting it changes battles

Every one of the 135 grid cells was redrawn with its own seed and gave the stored teams bit for bit, so each team's correction weight sits beside its 49 stored battle outcomes. On the random-order rows the weight and the win rate move together: Spearman +0.39 (linear) and +0.32 (per-field) within asks, at guidance 2. Full correction leaves about 2% effective teams, so read the dose curve's direction rather than its end point; the decile chart rests on about 96 teams per point. A weight does not say why a team is weak: teams whose sets were drawn long before their species are both the most bent and, from the order results, the weakest. The exact test on one Pokémon shows the mechanism: the lookahead keeps any ability some Fake Out user has, without weighting it by how much of the model's next move stays legal, so 17% of the probability moves. The tables cut 108 to 151 nats per team, but the training loss masks the same values, so the cut removes untrained mass and tracks win rate at only +0.10 and +0.04. Rejection without the lookahead, run since (#358), gives 0.410: 49% of the gap, against 84% from reweighting, whose 1-2% effective teams let a few heavy teams set the estimate.

4. The paper's assumption: one model, consistent across orders

TWISTER takes the model's per-field predictions as fixed and asks what the sampler does with them; it never checks that they come from one joint distribution (refuter, S11). Ours do not: the same held-out team scores 18.2 nats better species-first than moves-first on the per-field checkpoint and 12.3 on the linear one. So the tilt-corrected target is itself order-specific: a corrected random order is the model's own random-order distribution, not the species-first one.

5. Experiment-design lessons the paper's own evidence supports

Compute matching

“Although we report average generation times, our implementation is not optimized for performance.”appendix-eval.tex:41

TWISTER with 1 or 4 particles takes 1.4 to 2.1 times DINGO's time, and no method gets TWISTER's budget. Fair: give the uncorrected decoder and rejection sampling the same denoiser calls (8 times the samples for 8 particles) and plot distance against calls.

Cost of the fair version: 8 × 10,000 constrained samples per point; about 8 × the paper's sampling time

Seeds

“% We run all experiments once.” (commented out of the compiled paper)appendix-eval.tex:32

The compiled text gives no run count or seed. The only uncertainty is a split-half noise floor (method-eval.tex:9). Fair: three seeds per point with bootstrap intervals on the distance.

Cost of the fair version: 3 × the sampling; about 0 extra for the intervals

The judge

“We use pairwise total variation distance to measure the similarity between the two distributions.”method-eval.tex:7

Distance over token classes on a toy constraint ('a.*'), 3 of 6 models shown; JSON is scored only for validity, with an unnamed checker and no quality measure. Fair: all six models, and one task where the tilt could change answer quality.

Cost of the fair version: the other three models' points already exist; one quality benchmark

Text against algorithm

Main-text Algorithm 1 has its resampling step commented out (alg-twister.tex:68-84)method-algorithm.tex:16 · appendix-algorithm.tex:14

The prose and the appendix algorithm resample when the effective sample size drops below a threshold; the main algorithm never resamples, and no threshold value is reported.

Cost of the fair version: one sentence and one number

Baselines

“The original code is not publicly available.”appendix-eval.tex:23, 25

Both baselines are the authors' reimplementations, and TWISTER's own code is promised “upon acceptance” (mandatory.tex:11).

Cost of the fair version: release the code

6. The decoding rule, quoted from code

LLaDA's released sampler has no constraint at all. Ours puts the rules inside the model and then, in random order only, removes more at each draw without correcting. TWISTER is the only one of the three that corrects, and it releases no code. About two filled fields in five are not the model's top value in every row, so the samplers differ in what they remove, not in how greedy they are.

8. Adoption

Semantic Scholar lists one citer on 2026-11-15, DConstrain (arXiv 2610.30920), which mentions TWISTER in its introduction and related work and runs it nowhere. No released sampler changed its default: LLaDA's generate.py (9182493720) has no constraint, and the most-used constrained diffusion code, Mündler et al.'s, enforces validity and redraws without correcting. Nothing has replaced TWISTER because nothing has used it; the methods around it are shown faster or more often valid, never closer to the model's distribution at matched compute.
