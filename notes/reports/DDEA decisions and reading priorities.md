# Read DDEA as a search experiment

The DDEA authors use binary tournament parent selection, but the inspected public record does not establish why they chose two competitors. General tournament theory explains the tradeoff; it does not recover their private reasoning or prove that this setting is best. Your reading of KaMIS is supported: **on the three larger graph families, KaMIS returns larger mean independent sets with a 60-second budget**, while DDEA's reported times are substantially longer. The paper demonstrates a useful learned recombination framework and improvements over its diffusion baseline, rather than the strongest MIS solver among its tested methods. Our course retained the correct table values but needed clearer emphasis on KaMIS and more careful runtime wording. The citation review adds exactly two MED readings to the current list: Böther et al. on testing learned guidance, followed by selected Bengio et al. methodology sections. ## Binary tournaments are documented, their specific rationale is not

A binary tournament compares two sampled whole solutions and chooses the fitter one for a parent position. Repeat parent selection to obtain another parent. The word "binary" describes the two competitors, not the bit representation or two winners. DDEA states this rule alongside learned recombination, mutation, elitist replacement, and duplicate rejection. Its experiments vary operators, parent quality, population size, and graph size, but report no comparison of parent-selection rules or tournament sizes. ([Paper §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08627v1#S3), [experimental scope](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08627v1#S4))

The public history gives an implementation fact, not a reason. A commit dated 1 March 2025 changes defaults from four competitors to two. Its message and patch contain no supporting benchmark or diversity measurement. A separate code comment describes tournament-based survivor selection as less greedy than truncation and intended to help diversity. That routine chooses who remains in the combined parent–child population. It cannot be quoted as the authors' explanation for binary parent selection. ([Default-size change](https://github.com/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/commit/4b69753bfa5062578912b00a5cd7fde059b2e1b1), [survivor-selection comment](https://github.com/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/blob/ff1d9a8e58f6a7fcf92f9bd426f2e1b4853d7393/src/problems/mis/mis_ga.py#L526-L545))

The research, checked on 20 October 2026, covered the paper, repository documentation, five pages of returned commit messages, 46 commits touching the MIS genetic-algorithm file, and relevant patches. Public issue and pull-request endpoints returned empty arrays, the size-change commit had no returned comments, and repository metadata reported Discussions disabled at inspection. Private chats, experiment notebooks, and unpublished trials were unavailable. The conclusion is therefore bounded: no documented justification was found in the inspected public record. It is not a claim that the authors never considered alternatives. ([Repository history](https://github.com/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/commits/main/), [file history](https://github.com/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/commits/main/src/problems/mis/mis_ga.py), [issues](https://api.github.com/repos/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/issues?state=all&per_page=100), [pull requests](https://api.github.com/repos/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/pulls?state=all&per_page=100), [metadata](https://api.github.com/repos/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea))

The general explanation is simpler. Tournament size controls how strongly selection favors higher current fitness. A two-way comparison costs one comparison of stored scores and can select a solution below the population's best when that best solution is absent. Larger tournaments favor the best ranks more strongly under fixed sampling assumptions. Too much preference can narrow reproduction around current winners; too little can waste opportunities on weaker parents. These are standard considerations, not a measured explanation of DDEA's chosen setting. ([Miller and Goldberg, §§2.1–2.2](https://wpmedia.wolfram.com/sites/13/2018/02/09-3-2.pdf#page=3))

An illustrative calculation makes the qualification visible. With four distinct-fitness candidates and a uniformly sampled subset of k different competitors, the unique best wins with probability k/4. Binary selection gives 50%; a four-way tournament always selects the best. With independent draws allowing replacement, the binary probability becomes 1 − [3/4]² = 43.75%. Neither calculation identifies DDEA's complete sampling and tie conventions or establishes better final search quality. Comparing cached scores also does not itself run another battle. DDEA's fitness is exact set cardinality; Gaussian proposal noise is different from uncertainty in a team's measured win rate. ([Tournament definition and fitness-noise distinction](https://wpmedia.wolfram.com/sites/13/2018/02/09-3-2.pdf#page=3), [DDEA fitness](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08627v1#S3.SS1))

## KaMIS wins the larger quality and time comparisons

The compact comparison below uses the paper's reported mean sizes and runtime column T. KaMIS has a stated 60-second budget. The size percentages are calculations from rounded table means, using DDEA as the denominator. ([Tables 4 and 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=9))

| ER nodes | KaMIS mean size | KaMIS budget, s | DDEA mean size | DDEA reported T, s | KaMIS size advantage over DDEA |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 50 to 100 | 20.75 | 60 | 20.75 | 13.3 | Tie |
| 300 to 400 | 37.05 | 60 | 36.85 | 183.3 | 0.543% |
| 700 to 800 | 44.85 | 60 | 44.13 | 803.1 | 1.632% |
| 1,300 to 1,400 | 49.91 | 60 | 43.17 | 909.5 | 15.613% |

KaMIS has larger mean sets and less listed time than DDEA on each larger family. On the OOD family it wins both quantities against every displayed method. The exceptions matter: on the smallest family, Gurobi matches 20.75 in a reported 0.1 seconds. On the two larger in-distribution families, one-shot DIFUSCO runs in 8.7 and 43.6 seconds, faster than the KaMIS budget, but returns smaller sets. KaMIS is therefore not universally fastest at every displayed endpoint. ([Full benchmark tables](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=9))

The headline percentages answer different questions. DDEA's approximately 3.9% and 7.5% larger in-distribution sets are relative to Gurobi. Its gains over timed DIFUSCO, calculated from rounded cells, are about 0.245% and 0.318%. The OOD 11.6% is relative to timed DIFUSCO's 38.69, not KaMIS's 49.91. Using KaMIS as the reference, DDEA's OOD mean is approximately 13.5% smaller. That differs from saying KaMIS is 15.6% larger because the denominator changes. These ratios of means are not mean per-instance percentage gaps. ([Results and denominators](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=9))

The in-distribution test sets contain 128 graphs each; OOD contains 42 larger ER graphs. Tables 4 and 5 provide no uncertainty intervals or clear repeated-seed counts. Their means do not establish a win on every instance or statistical significance. The paper does not fully document the table T averaging and preprocessing boundary, and the 60-second KaMIS budget does not identify when it first found its final set. Diffusion uses an A100 80 GB GPU, while other experiments use the reported Xeon cluster. Equal elapsed time is not equal hardware cost; offline demonstration generation and network training are also outside these solving comparisons. ([Setup](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=6), [timing description](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=8), [limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08627v1#page=10))

KaMIS also needs a precise description. It is a software family, and ReduMIS is itself an evolutionary solver operating with graph reductions. The accessed author wrapper selects ReduMIS for unweighted instances and pins a KaMIS revision. This is code-level corroboration, not proof that the accessed author revision generated the paper's tables: the paper does not pin its source commit. KaMIS is also distinct from DDEA's parent-restricted Gurobi recombination teacher. ([Pinned KaMIS description](https://github.com/KarlsruheMIS/KaMIS/blob/791334ef6aebb2ccf005316258c043cca58923a0/README.md), [accessed author wrapper](https://github.com/jsalvasoler/difusco_ddea/blob/ff1d9a8e58f6a7fcf92f9bd426f2e1b4853d7393/src/mis_benchmark_framework/solvers/kamis.py), [teacher construction](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08627v1#S3.SS3))

Read Böther et al., *What's Wrong with Deep Learning in Tree Search for Combinatorial Optimization*, ICLR 2022, first. Sections 3.1–3.2 test whether learned guidance contributes beyond reductions, local search, and other search machinery. In the studied tree-search configurations, random values can match GCN guidance once those components are active. The same paper finds a useful GNN contribution in a separate solver, so its lesson is how to isolate learning's contribution, not that learning is always useless. ([Primary record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10494), [full paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494))

For counter-team search, the proposed transfer is an ablation: compare learned and random legal proposals while controlling repair, selection, incumbent handling, and the established battle budget. Then test those other components separately. The outcome remains higher validated battle win rate under the existing evaluation protocol, not a closer match to a teacher or a better training loss. This is a proposed application of the paper's evaluation logic; deterministic MIS experiments do not establish robustness to noisy battles. ([Böther et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494), [project objective](</Users/ramiismael/Documents/yakumsi-vault/Personal Project/Using Advance method search in the whole search of possible vgc pokemon format to find the right counter to a pokemon team/What counts as a good counter pokemon team.md>))

The second addition is Bengio, Lodi, and Prouvost, *Machine Learning for Combinatorial Optimization: A Methodological Tour d'Horizon*, EJOR 2021, available as arXiv:1811.06128. Read §§3.2, 4.2, 4.3, and 4.6 selectively. They distinguish where learning enters an optimizer, the learning objective from actual optimization performance, held-out generalization, and offline learning costs. This gives a framework for judging a proposal operator; it is not a ready-made categorical team decoder or stochastic battle-search algorithm. ([Primary preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.06128), [full paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06128), [journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2020.07.063))

## Conclusion

The useful next decision is whether a learned proposal earns its cost inside your search. That requires a controlled comparison under the project's established opponents, evaluation protocol, and budget, with fresh validation of the resulting teams. A tournament-size change answers a separate question and should not be credited for a proposal improvement. DDEA provides a design to test; the two selected readings help determine whether its learned component contributes to the outcome you care about. Neither a public default nor a successful MIS comparison substitutes for that local evidence. ([DDEA experimental scope](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08627v1#S4), [evaluation ablations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494), [methodological distinctions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06128))
