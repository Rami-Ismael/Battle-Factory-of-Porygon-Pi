Fictitious play is a mixture, not a mean

The opponent in FP is one frozen checkpoint, sampled — not one network holding the average of all of them. The word "average" is doing damage, and it hides the thing that actually separates FP from vanilla self-play: frozen versus live.

Lesson 0008 · 2026-08-23 · ~10 minutes · source: Zhang et al., A Survey on Self-play Methods in Reinforcement Learning (🟢 arXiv 2408.01072, v4 2025-10-18), §3.1–§3.4, §5.2 · builds on lesson 0002 · mission

Why this, now. You flagged the mistake yourself: people say fictitious play "trains against the average of all past versions," and that sentence is in your own paper note. It is wrong in the way that matters — it makes FP sound like one opponent when it is many, and it never mentions that they are frozen. After this lesson you should be able to (1) say what the survey's four framework components are and write any arm as one row of Σ, (2) state the three things "average" could mean and which one FP is, and (3) answer your own open question in Why Self-Play is consider Multi-Agent.md — does a frozen opponent make the environment stationary?

2. The mistake: three things "average" can mean

"FP trains against the average of all past versions" is ambiguous across three completely different objects. Only one is FP.

ReadingIs it FP?
AAverage of the weights. Take θ(1)…θ(m−1), mean them, play the resulting network.No. This is parameter averaging (EMA / SWA / Polyak). The mean of parameters is not the mean of behaviours — averaging two networks, one that always leads Trick Room and one that never does, does not give you a network that leads it half the time. Useful as a deployment trick (the Generals.io paper in your reading list steals exactly this); it is not an opponent-selection scheme.
BAverage of the behaviours, distilled into one net. Keep a reservoir of your own past actions and supervised-train a second network to imitate them.Only in NFSP. This is real and it is Heinrich & Silver's average policy network — but it is a specific implementation choice for representing the mixture compactly, not what FP is. Your arms do not do this.
CA mixed strategy: a distribution over frozen policies. Before each battle, draw one past checkpoint uniformly and play that one, whole.Yes. This is the survey's FP row: σm+1,n = 1/(m−1) for n ≤ m−1, 0 otherwise. Uniform over every earlier member of Π.

Where the word "average" legitimately comes from: Brown's original fictitious play says each player best-responds to the empirical frequency of the opponent's past play. That empirical frequency is an average — an average over the distribution, not over the parameters. And you never instantiate it: you sample from it. Play 1,000 battles against a uniform draw from Π and the opponent you faced, in aggregate, is that average. The average is a fact about the batch, never about any single game and never about any single network.

Say it this way. "Fictitious play holds a population of frozen past selves — each one a snapshot taken after some number of battles and never updated again — and samples one uniformly as the opponent for each battle. The 'average' is the mixture you're sampling from, not a network you built."

3. What the mistake hides: frozen versus live

Collapsing FP to "an average" loses the load-bearing contrast. It is not one opponent vs. many. It is an opponent that is still receiving gradient updates vs. opponents that will never change again.

Now your open question. "Does a frozen agent make the environment stationary or non-stationary?" The precise answer:

  • Conditioned on a sampled opponent, yes. For the duration of one battle against frozen π₃, the transition and reward process the learner faces is a fixed MDP. That is why frozen opponents are a variance-reduction and credit-assignment win, not just an anti-forgetting one.
  • Marginally over the draw, still yes — for as long as Σ's row is fixed. A fixed mixture over fixed policies is itself a fixed stochastic environment.
  • Across appends, no — but the drift is bounded. Every snapshot changes the row from uniform-over-(m−1) to uniform-over-m; the distribution moves by O(1/m) and shrinks as training goes on. That is what "piecewise stationary" actually buys you.

Be precise about the comparison. Vanilla self-play is also piecewise-stationary in practice — the survey says so itself in §3.2.2: "Typically, each player's policy is fixed, and samples are collected into a buffer… This allows the agent to leverage past experiences and maintain more stationary training." Your PPO loop freezes the weights for the whole COLLECT phase and only then updates. So the honest statement is not "vanilla shifts every gradient step, FP does not" — it is that both are piecewise stationary, and the pieces differ in size and in how far each boundary moves. Vanilla's piece is one PPO iteration and the jump across it is unbounded; FP's piece lasts until the next append and the jump is O(1/m) and shrinking. That is the claim you can defend.

What the survey does not give you. §5.2 Non-stationarity of the Environment is one paragraph. It states the problem — "the strategies of the opponent players evolve as training progresses… creating a non-stationary environment" — and gestures at opponent modelling as future work. It contains no analysis of piecewise stationarity and no bound. Everything above §3 of this lesson is derived from the MSS equations (5)–(8), not quoted from §5.2. Cite it that way.

4b. Why AlphaZero wins on the simplest row — and why that does not transfer

Two ingredients, neither of which is opponent selection:

  1. MCTS is the real ORACLE. Every move is chosen by search over the network, and the network is regressed toward the search's visit counts. Search is a policy-improvement operator: its output is reliably better than the raw network, so the training target improves on its own regardless of who the opponent is. §3.2.2 flags this coupling directly — "vanilla self-play is frequently used in conjunction with Monte Carlo Tree Search." Your arms have no search. Vanilla self-play with PPO and no improvement operator is a materially weaker object than the thing that beat Lee Sedol, and the ablation separating them has never been run in your setting.
  2. Go is transitive. §3.2.2, verbatim: vanilla self-play "is effective in transitive games, but it can lead the agent to cyclic learning patterns in non-transitive games. It may also lead to a nonstationary problem, causing the system to get stuck in a local optimum." On a ladder, "fight your newest self" is optimal because your newest self genuinely is your hardest opponent. VGC is not a ladder — non-transitivity is the reason your evaluation is a cross-play matrix plus Alpha-Rank instead of a single number.

Both ingredients are missing in your setup, so AlphaZero is evidence for running the FP / δ-uniform / gated arms, not against. Compare §4.3.2: the systems in genuinely non-transitive games do not run a pure row either — OpenAI Five is 80% vanilla / 20% PFSP, and Honor of Kings 1v1 is δ-uniform.

5. The four branches — and the one you are not considering

§3 splits self-play into four families. Your three arms all live in the first one.

  1. Traditional self-play (§3.2) — vanilla, FP, δ-uniform, PFSP. Π is a checkpoint zoo; MSS is a hand-written rule over history.
  2. PSRO series (§3.3) — DO, PSRO, variants, R-NaD. MSS is a game solver run on the measured payoff matrix. This is where your cross-play matrix stops being a report and becomes an input.
  3. Ongoing-training (§3.4) — FTW, NeuPL, Simplex-NeuPL. All policies train at once; nothing is frozen. NeuPL is the interesting one for you: instead of K stored checkpoints it is one network conditioned on which population member it is meant to be, so skills transfer across members instead of being relearned. At poke-env throughput, "no checkpoint zoo, and basic play is learned once" is not a small claim.
  4. Regret minimisation (§3.5) — CFR and variants. This is where your existing Neller & Lanctot CFR primer sits, and where the imperfect-information side of VGC (open team sheets, hidden sets) is actually addressed.

The survey's other half is empirical: §4.1 board games, §4.2 card games, §4.3 video games. §4.3.1 StarCraft II is your closest read — it is where PFSP got famous, and where the "exploiter" idea lives that you have already ruled out.

In fictitious play, what does the learner's opponent look like in a single battle?

Reading C in §2. A is parameter averaging, a different technique. B is NFSP's average policy network — a way to compress the mixture, not the definition of FP. The average is a property of the batch of battles, never of any one game.

The sharpest difference between vanilla self-play and fictitious play is…

§3. "One vs. many" is a consequence, not the mechanism — and it is exactly what the "average" phrasing erases. Both arms use the same ORACLE (PPO); only the MSS row differs (§1, §4).

You freeze a snapshot after every 10,000 battles and sample uniformly. Is the learner's environment stationary?

§3. A fixed mixture over fixed policies is a fixed environment; appending a member moves the row from uniform-over-(m−1) to uniform-over-m. The learner changing is not what makes the environment non-stationary — the opponent distribution is. Note option c is wrong for a second reason: batched vanilla self-play is piecewise stationary too, just with a smaller piece and an unbounded jump.

Which single component of the survey's framework distinguishes vanilla / FP / δ-uniform / PFSP from each other?

§1 and §4. All four run the same approximate-best-response oracle over the same growing checkpoint population; only the MSS — the rule turning the performance matrix into an opponent distribution — changes. That is why they are one code path with a flag.

δ-uniform self-play with δ = 1 is equivalent to…

§4. δ = 0 keeps the whole history (FP); δ = 1 keeps only the newest member (vanilla). The parameter interpolates between the two arms you were treating as separate algorithms.

Which AlphaGo-family system does the survey describe with the fictitious-play MSS, Eq. (6)?

§4a. AlphaGo's stage-2 policy network is "refined by competing against a randomly selected historical version pρ⁻, similar to the MSS shown in Equ. (6)." AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero and MuZero are all Eq. (5). The family is four rows, not one.

§4a — "the only difference between AlphaZero and AlphaGo Zero is that AlphaZero utilizes the newly updated network without the validation process." That gate is the same promotion rule your "double oracle" arm uses, and the successor paper judged it not worth the validation games.

Why is "AlphaZero proves vanilla self-play works" a bad argument for your pure-SP arm?

§4b. The survey: vanilla self-play "is effective in transitive games, but it can lead the agent to cyclic learning patterns in non-transitive games."
