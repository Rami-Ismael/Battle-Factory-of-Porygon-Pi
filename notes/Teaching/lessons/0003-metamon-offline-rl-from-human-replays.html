Metamon: offline RL from human replays

Behaviour cloning is the floor. A filtered BC update is the cheapest step above it. Self-play data is a lever that can also pull you into playing yourself.

Lesson 0003 · 2026-08-19 · ~14 minutes · cheat sheet · context-window figure · glossary · mission · serves the battle-policy experiment (BC / SP / FP / DO arms) and the vault note Does pretraining on singles replays make a better VGC doubles battle policy.md · previous: 0002

Why this, now. Lesson 0002 left one box unfilled: your BC arm is not a self-play arm — so where does it live, and what is the published evidence that it is worth building first? Metamon (🟢 Grigsby, Xie, Sasek, Zheng & Zhu, RLC 2025) is that evidence, for singles. After this lesson you should be able to (1) place Metamon's whole pipeline on the five-symbol loop from 0002, (2) read its one actor-loss equation and say which row is BC and which rows are offline RL, and (3) write the critic's TD loss and say where its advantage enters the actor, and (4) name the exact failure their realistic self-play run hit — because your cross-play matrix can hide the same failure.

1. The claim, in their words

"We develop a pipeline to reconstruct the first-person perspective of an agent from logs saved from the third-person perspective of a spectator, thereby unlocking a dataset of real human battles spanning more than a decade that grows larger every day. … We study a progression from imitation learning to offline RL and offline fine-tuning on self-play data … The resulting agents outperform a recent LLM Agent approach and a strong heuristic search engine. While playing anonymously in online battles against humans, our best agents climb to rankings inside the top 10% of active players." 🟢 arXiv 2504.04395, abstract.

2. Where BC sits on the loop from Lesson 0002

Recall the five symbols: Π (population), σ (opponent sampling), ORACLE (training step), EVAL → 𝒫, MSS. Self-play methods differ by MSS. Offline learning from human replays skips the loop entirely on the first pass: there is no Π of your own checkpoints and no σ — the "opponents" are whoever the human in the replay faced, frozen in the dataset. The ORACLE is a supervised update on a fixed dataset D. That is why the survey never lists BC: it is not a self-play method; it is a starting policy that a self-play loop can later be seeded with.

Metamon's formal framing (Sec. 4) makes the opponent part of the environment — "we consider our opponent's choices part of the environment's unknown transition function T(st+1 | st, at, πo)" — and the objective is the expected return over a distribution of opponent policies and teams, p(πo, co). The sequence model conditions on the whole battle so far and is trusted to infer (πo, co) implicitly. (Figure: what is and is not in the context window.) Then the key sentence that makes this a dataset paper:

"We will be using the offline dataset (D) from Section 3 to approximate the expectations in Eq. (1), which assumes that the distribution of teams and playstyles across history is identical to that of the current game. This is false, but it may be close enough, particularly in the optimized world of Early Gen OU." 🟢 Sec. 4.

Translate to your vocabulary: their dataset plays the role your meta plays — a distribution over opponent teams and policies — and they state the same assumption you make when you treat the collected meta-team list as the thing to beat. Theirs is a distribution over history; yours is over a dated snapshot. Both are approximations of "the ladder right now".

3. One equation, four training objectives

Everything they train is one actor loss with two knobs (Sec. 4, Eq. 2):

Lactor = Eτ∼D [ (1/T) Σt ( −w(ht, at) · log π(at | ht)  −  λ · Ea∼π[Q(ht, a)] ) ]

Term one is behaviour cloning re-weighted by w; it can only ever raise the probability of actions that humans actually took. Term two is the ordinary off-policy actor update — "risks overestimating the value of OOD actions when used offline". The advantage is the critic's: A(h,a) = Q(h,a) − Ea'∼π[Q(h,a')].

Drive the weights yourself. One timestep, four legal actions, the critic's Q for each, the action the human clicked. Watch what happens to wBinary the moment the human's action falls below the policy's own expected value.

Two findings worth memorising, both from Sec. 5.2 and Fig. 9: "RL updates significantly outperform the pure-BC Transformers, but there is little difference between the many RL variants considered." And model size helps BC more visibly than it helps RL. So: the step from IL to any filtered row is the one that pays; which filter is secondary.

What the critic is for here

Nothing in the Exp/Binary rows ever acts on the critic's arg-max. The critic is a data filter. The reward that trains it is R = rhp + ½ rstat + rfaint + 100·rwin (App. E.1) — win/loss dominates, shaping exists "to help the offline filter w … learn to assign unique weights over short horizons". They admit a side effect: agents "cling to life in clearly lost positions by using recovery moves".

3b. The other equation — how the critic is trained

Eq. (2) is the actor only. Metamon does not print the critic loss; it defers to AMAGO ("we refer the reader to the AMAGO implementation used throughout our experiments"). The equation lives in 🟢 AMAGO, ICLR 2024 (App. A, Eq. 2–3) and is restated with the sequence-model state in 🟢 AMAGO-2, NeurIPS 2024 (Sec. 3, Eq. 1):

yt = rt + γ · Q̄(ht+1, a′ ∼ π̄(ht+1))     LCritic(t) = ( Q(ht, at) − yt )²

Plain one-step TD regression on the replay transitions; Q̄, π̄ are slow target copies of the heads (τ = 0.004), never of the Transformer. The joint update is Σt Lactor(t) + λ·LCritic(t) with λ = 10 (Table 3 "TD Loss Coeff"), and the critic's parameters are blocked from minimising the actor term — AMAGO-2: "By preventing the parameters of Q from minimizing LActor, we can update the full architecture with one step of the joint loss."

SynRL-V2's swap. Regression → two-hot classification (AMAGO-2 Eq. 3): the critic emits softmax probabilities QB over B bins b, recovers Q = QBTb, and the target y becomes a two-hot label on the two adjacent bins bracketing it:

LCritic-Ind(t) = − twohotB(yt)T log QB(ht, at)

Metamon Table 4: 96 bins spaced evenly on [−110, 110] — just wider than the ±100 win/loss reward. Effect they report (Fig. 25): a sharper critic, hence a far more pessimistic 𝟙[A>0] filter.

How the two equations meet

A(h,a) = Q(h,a) − Ea'∼πQ(h,a'), with Q the ensemble mean. That advantage is the only place the critic touches Eq. (2) when λ = 0. TD trains Q on the replays; Q's advantage decides which replay decisions the BC term learns from. Nothing else.

4. Putting self-play back in — and the failure they hit

Having a ladder-capable policy, they re-enter the loop from Lesson 0002: deploy checkpoints on a local Showdown ladder, add those trajectories to D, retrain. Now Π and σ exist. Their worry is exactly the distribution assumption in §2: new self-play data shifts the implied p(πo, co) away from the real ladder. Their answer is odd and worth keeping:

"One approach would be to try and generate data that is clearly different from the original set so that when conditioned on a real battle, our model's implicit estimate of p(πo, co | τ0:i) should be unchanged at small i. We let a mix of checkpoints from all our agents compete on a locally hosted PS ladder, playing with teams from the Variety Team Set. By prioritizing diversity over realism, we hope this data will cover replay reconstruction failures and improve model-free learning of Pokémon's stochastic transitions without biasing estimates of human teams and strategies." 🟢 Sec. 5.3.

So the self-play teams are deliberately unrealistic (1,000 procedurally generated teams per gen/tier) — a watermark that tells the model "this is not a human". Then they tested the cautious version against the obvious one:

The trap, named in Lesson 0002's terms

SynRL-V1+SP is vanilla self-play with a constant σ pointed at the latest checkpoint. The cross-play matrix 𝒫 said it improved. The ladder said it had learned to model its own p(πo) rather than the human one. This is the concrete reason your cross-play ranking of BC / SP / FP / DO needs at least one column that is not one of your own arms — a human-replay opponent or a fixed external engine — or it will reward exactly this failure.

6. Retrieval practice

From memory. Options are equal length on purpose.

In Metamon's Eq. (2), which row is plain behaviour cloning?

IL is w ≡ 1, λ = 0: every human decision weighted equally, no critic involvement in the actor. Exp and Binary are the filtered-BC rows; Binary+MaxQ adds the off-data actor term.

What is the critic actually used for in the Exp and Binary rows?

With λ = 0 the policy only ever raises probability on actions in the dataset; the critic's advantage decides how much each human decision counts (or, for Binary, whether it counts at all).

Why were the self-play teams deliberately unrealistic?

Sec. 5.3: data "clearly different from the original set so that when conditioned on a real battle, our model's implicit estimate of p(πo, co | τ0:i) should be unchanged". A watermark against self-modelling.

SynRL-V1+SP got better in cross-play but not on the ladder. In Lesson 0002's vocabulary, what went wrong?

Vanilla self-play: σ samples the latest checkpoint, so the implied opponent distribution becomes the model itself. 𝒫 (cross-play) cannot see this; only an external column — humans — can.

Which comparison did the paper find made the biggest difference?

Sec. 5.2: "RL updates significantly outperform the pure-BC Transformers, but there is little difference between the many RL variants considered." Size helped BC more than RL; two-hot mattered only for V2's filter pessimism.

Where does Metamon's dataset take the role your meta plays?

Eq. (1) takes an expectation over p(πo, co); D approximates it, under the stated (false but "close enough") assumption that history ≈ the current ladder. Your meta-team list approximates the same object from a dated snapshot.
