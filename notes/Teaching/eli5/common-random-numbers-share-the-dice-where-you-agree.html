Share the dice where you agree

To find out which of two moves is better in a world full of luck, don't test them on different luck — test them on the same luck. But only for the stretch of the future where the two moves behave identically. Sharing luck where they disagree can make things worse.

1 · You have to pick, and the future rolls dice

You're playing a board game. Two moves are legal. You can't see how the game ends, so you do the obvious thing: imagine finishing the game, over and over, once starting with move A and once with move B. Write down the score each time. Average them. Take the higher average.

That's the whole recipe the paper is about. The imagined games are called rollouts.

2 · The dice are louder than the moves

A averaged 7. B averaged 5. Was that the move, or was that the dice? A might have drawn a lucky run of imagined games and B an unlucky one. You genuinely cannot tell.

The usual fix is brute force: imagine thousands more games until the luck averages out. That is exactly the thing you can't afford.

3 · The fix: hand both moves the same dice

Roll the dice first and write every roll down in a notebook. Then replay the whole future for move A reading from that notebook — and replay it for move B reading from the same notebook.

Now the two imagined games had identical luck. Whatever gap is left over is the move. That's it. That's common random numbers.

In the paper this is dressed up as "sample a deterministic MDP": before anything runs, you write down one outcome for every combination of where-you-are, what-you-do and which-turn-it-is. The notebook is the whole world's luck, decided in advance.

Sharing the notebook is a bid to make that middle piece big. In kid words: if both plans are having a good day at the same time, the difference between them barely moves. Nothing here is a cheat — the averages stay honest. Only the wobble changes.

5 · The nasty surprise: sometimes sharing makes it worse

Here is the paper's own counterexample, shrunk to candy. One coin flip sends you to Room Left or Room Right. Plan A does great in Left and badly in Right. Plan B is the exact opposite.

Count it out. With one shared coin, the gap is +8 or −8 — nothing else. With two separate coins, the gap can also land on 0 (both do well, or both do badly), and it does so half the time. Separate coins are calmer. Sharing made the answer exactly twice as jumpy.

Sharing dice is a bet, not a law. When one plan's good luck is the other plan's bad luck, the bet loses.

6 · The safe rule: share only where the two plans do the same thing

Look again at what went wrong. The coin hurt because the two plans reacted to it differently — they made different choices in the rooms. So the paper draws a line down the future:

Why is the right-hand side safe? Because once both imagined games land on the same square, and they're both following the same habit, and they're both reading the same dice — they are no longer two games. They're literally one game, written down twice. Two copies of one thing move together perfectly. That can only push the middle piece of the arithmetic up.

And if they never land on the same square? Then they were reading different dice all along, and it's a wash. Either way you never lose.

That's the whole theorem: split the dice where the plans differ, share them where the plans agree — and you are guaranteed to be no worse off than using separate dice everywhere. Usually much better.

7 · The best part: you get that split for free

It sounds like a fussy condition — "the two plans must behave identically after step d". It isn't. It's how rollout planning already works.

You're comparing move A against move B. They differ for exactly one step — the first one. After that, both imagined games are finished off by the same lazy habit, because that's the point of a rollout: you don't think hard about the far future, you just play it out. So d = 1, handed to you.

8 · How you actually do it: you name the dice

There is no new machinery. No notebook gets stored. You just label each random number with a name, and identical names are made to produce identical rolls.

Add the plan's name to the label and the two plans get different rolls. Leave it off and they get the same rolls. That one on/off switch, flipped at step d, is the entire implementation.

9 · Does it work?

Three tests: a tiny made-up world, a task about paying out a pension fund year by year, and 5000 games of the board game Ludo. Shared dice win — and they win biggest when you can only afford a handful of imagined games, which is the situation you're actually in.

Two honest gaps the authors name themselves. First, in Ludo the reckless version — share everything, the one that has no guarantee — did just as well as the safe version. The theorem doesn't cover the tree-growing search they used there, and they say so rather than papering over it. Second, nobody knows how to find the split point d automatically; you have to know where the two plans stop disagreeing.

10 · Why it sits in this vault

Half of this transfers, and half of it doesn't — and the halves are worth keeping straight.

What you can take today: pair the opponent. Battle 1 of team A and battle 1 of team B face the same sampled meta team, played by the same opponent brain with the same decision seed. A hard opponent is hard for both, which is exactly the "jump together" piece. That's classical paired comparison — free, and it needs nothing from the simulator.

What you can't: the theorem itself. Two candidate teams disagree from turn one, so there is no stretch where they "do the same thing" to share dice over. And Showdown's randomness comes off one running tape rather than being labelled per event, so a shared battle seed stops lining up the moment the two battles diverge.

The verdict, quoted from the vault: "(a) The CRN idea for team-vs-team battle comparisons — MED. … (b) The paper's specific theoretical contribution (X_DD, Theorem 2) for this pipeline as it exists today — LOW."
