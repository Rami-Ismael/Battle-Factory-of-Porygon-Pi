The second loop: what repeats inside a denoising step

A discrete diffusion model fills a team one step at a time. A looped model also repeats a few layers inside each step, before it commits anything. This article teaches that idea on our own team model, then sorts the 2026 papers by what they repeat, what they keep, and who decides when to stop.

Draft · 2 November 2026 · No looped model has run on teams. Numbers come from the papers named beside them, or from read-only measurements on the non-looped model. Goes further than the 31 October explainer.

A step changes the input

The from-scratch discrete diffusion model trained only on valid Pokémon teams writes a team as 84 fields. Each Pokémon has 14: species, ability, item, four moves, Stat Alignment and six Stat Points. Sampling starts with all 84 set to [MASK].

A denoising step runs the whole network once, predicts every hidden field, and writes one of them as a token. The next step reads that token as input. Our sampler takes 84 steps, one field each, species first. That is already a loop, and the papers call it the outer loop.

A loop changes only the hidden state

A looped model adds an inner loop inside each step. It splits the layers into a head that runs once, a looped block that runs several times with the same weights, and a tail that runs once. The number of repeats is the loop count, written S. The tokens stay fixed during the loop. Only the hidden vectors change, so masked fields can read each other's half-formed predictions before anything is committed.

Our model has 6 layers and 5.5 million parameters. If it looped layers 1 and 2, as the looped masked diffusion paper does, one step would cost 1 + 2S + 3 layer runs. At S = 1 that is today's model. At S = 4 it computes like a 12-layer network and stores 6 layers of weights.

Repeated layers give masked fields a place to work

A step already reruns every layer, so why repeat them inside it? The looped masked diffusion paper (LoopMDM) answers with a small test. It forces a one-layer model to fill Sudoku in fixed left-to-right order, so the order cannot help. Without looping the model solves 10.9% of boards exactly. On one shown board, errors fall from 24 to 7 to 0 at loop counts 1, 2 and 3.

The authors read this as masked positions acting as a workspace: early guesses get revised against other unresolved cells before any is written down. They also measure attention between masked positions on language data, and it rises with the loop count. This is the result closest to our setup, because our order is also fixed and it commits species while every set is still hidden.

Six ways to loop

You named three papers, and each one answers a different question. LoopMDM asks whether looping pays in masked diffusion. ALoDLM asks whether every token needs the same depth. The looped uniform diffusion paper asks whether looping survives when the model cannot see which tokens are corrupted, and how to spend loops. Figure 2 places them with the rest of the field, by what each one changes.

The first three panels change the work inside a step, and the fourth changes what crosses between steps. The pure self-conditioning paper calls those two directions complementary. I think that is the most useful split to remember: looping inside a step spends more compute on one input, and carrying state stops the next step from throwing that compute away.

What a loop cannot fix

The recursive masked diffusion paper (R-MDM) separates two errors. Fidelity error means one field's prediction is wrong by itself. Dependence error means each prediction is fine alone, but fields committed together do not fit. A loop can reduce the first. At a fixed state and commit set, it cannot change the second. On puzzles with more than one valid answer, the paper attributes 83% to 88% of same-step clashes at 5 loops to dependence. Only a commit schedule with fewer fields per step lowers those.

Our model makes this concrete. Read the six item predictions at once and commit them together, and 71% of 2,000 sampled teams repeat an item, which Item Clause forbids. The deployed sampler repeats none, because it commits one field per step and applies the clause. So the usual selling point of looped diffusion, generating with fewer steps, buys little here unless we also want many fields per step.

One thing this measurement turned up. On MB607, with only species visible, each Pokémon's Stat Alignment keeps about 9 effective options and its Defense Stat Points about 19, whichever of the six it is. Item predictions on a team differ by a total variation of 0.21 on average, over 40 real teams, while real item choices differ between species by 0.93 across 591 tournament teams. The two numbers are not the same comparison, and most of the 297,954 training teams are not tournament teams, so this may reflect the training mix. It may also connect to the position-embedding question in issue #78. I have not investigated it further. Looping has costs the headlines skip

A loop count is not free to raise. Figure 3 shows one model's full sweep. It is an image model, the representation-supervised looped model (LoopREG), used here because it reports diversity beside quality at every loop count. Quality improves past the trained count of 3, peaks at 5, then turns. Recall, which measures how much of the real data the samples cover, sits below the non-looped model's 0.640 from count 3 onward.

Other costs show up across the papers. A looped model run at loop count 1 is worse than a non-looped one: 124.6 against 108.5 perplexity on one held-out text set in the looped masked paper, and worse on every data set in the uniform paper. Matching training compute means a looped model sees fewer training tokens. ALoDLM warns that its speed depends on the input and can reverse on unfamiliar domains. In the image paper, an extra loop without representation supervision made quality worse, from 34.45 to 46.41.

What this could mean for the team model

The papers measure language or puzzle quality, and our measures are different: the ask-versus-got slope, win rate, and how many new line-ups appear. None of the papers can say what looping does to those. Three points follow from the reading.

The cheapest test is the first panel. Loop layers 1 and 2, draw the loop count at random in training, and change nothing in the sampler. Training compute is where the headline savings sit. Sampling is not our bottleneck, because battles take far longer than filling a team.

The per-token idea has real variation to work with. Effective options is the exponential of the entropy of a prediction, counting legal values only. When a field commits, averaged over 256 sampled teams, an ability has about 1.2 and an item about 8. Whether that gap is difficulty the model could resolve, or just how many reasonable choices exist, is an open question. More compute cannot shrink honest uncertainty.

Diversity has to sit beside the score. Figure 3 is an image model, so it proves nothing about teams, but it is the same kind of trade this project keeps measuring. A loop test that reports only the slope would miss it.
