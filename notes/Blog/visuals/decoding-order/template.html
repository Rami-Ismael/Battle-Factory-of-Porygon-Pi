Fill the species first

I tried five orders for filling in a team's 48 fields. The default, all six species first, still makes the strongest teams. Every other order lost 0.06 to 0.09 of win rate (the share of battles won against the top-50 meta teams), and only some of them got more varied teams in return.

The discrete diffusion model trained from scratch on valid Pokémon teams builds a team the way you'd fill in a form with every box blank. Each pass of the network fills one blank field (a species, an ability, an item, one of four moves, or a Stat Alignment, the Pokémon's choice of nature such as Adamant) while seeing everything filled so far. Six Pokémon with eight fields each make 48 passes. Stat Points are not among the 48: they are copied from a training team of the same species when the finished team is written out.

Decoding order is the order the blanks get filled. The model has always used species-first order: all six species, then abilities and items, then moves, then Stat Alignments. This experiment asked whether a different order makes better teams, more varied teams, or teams that land closer to the win rate I ask for (the ask).

Five orders, one model

Nothing about the model changed between arms: the same networks, the same asks, the same seeds, guidance weight 2 and temperature 1. Only the order of the blanks moved. Pick an order and step through how it filled one freshly sampled team at ask 0.5.

Two things stand out on the boards. Random order usually draws part of a Pokémon's set before its species (7 times in 8, by chance), and by the time the species comes up, no species may fit what was drawn. Confidence order fills, at each pass, the blank the model is surest about; in the example above it fills four moves before any species.

Only two orders reliably make legal teams

For each cell (one order, one ask, one seed) I drew teams until 64 passed Showdown's validator, giving up at 1,280 tries.

The model checks each new field only against fields already filled. Species-first order picks the species while nothing else constrains it, then builds a set around it; all 1,152 of its tries were legal. Random order often draws part of a set before its species, and then the earlier picks may already rule out every species. Confidence order's teams fail far less often (63% legal against under 1%); I did not count its dead ends separately. Species-first order makes the strongest teams

The trade: more variety, weaker teams

Win rate alone can hide a trade. An order can lose strength because it spreads out into more kinds of team. So here is each order's variety against its win rate.

No order follows the ask better

This experiment started from a gap: when I ask for a 0.531 win rate (the elite cut, the mean win rate of a top-50 meta team against the other 49), the teams fall short. If one order let the ask steer the species, achieved win rate would track the ask more closely.

Remasking raises legality, not enough to rescue random order

Random order's teams fail at dead ends: a Pokémon's set drawn before its species leaves no species that fits. Remasking masks some filled fields again before each pass, and that pass redraws them along with its new field. A field redrawn after its Pokémon's species is filled is checked against that species, so remasking can repair a dead end. When a remasking pass runs into a dead end, the team gets back its fields from before the pass and draws only its new field; that pass is undone.

The follow-up tried four remasking settings, all on random order; Figure 8 lists them. They differ in which filled fields are masked again (the least-likely ones, drawn with the lowest probability, or random ones), how many (10% or 20% of the filled fields), and the number of passes: 48, with one new field per pass, or 96, with a new field on every other pass and only redraws on the passes between. Before the run I set the bar for rescuing random order: its share of legal tries must rise by more than noise and reach at least half (code/docs/remask-random-order.md).

The lookahead makes most tries legal, and the teams get weaker

Remasking tries to repair a dead end after it happens. The lookahead stops it from happening. For every Pokémon whose species is still masked, the sampler keeps the list of species that still fit that Pokémon's visible fields and Species Clause (each species at most once per team). Before each draw, it removes every value that would empty that list, and the field is drawn from the values left. Species-first order never needs it: every species is drawn before its set, so species-first sampling with the lookahead on is identical, bit for bit.

The lookahead follow-up ran three orders with it on: random order with one or two fields per pass, and confidence order, with the same model, asks, seeds and battles as above. Before the run I set two bars (code/docs/lookahead-legality.md). The lookahead fixes an order's legality if the order's legal share rises by more than noise and reaches at least 90%. An order with the lookahead matches species-first order if its win rate is within noise of species-first's.

        How the numbers were made
        • Arms: five decoding orders, each at six asks (7/24, 0.3, 8/24, 0.5, 0.531, 13/24) and three seeds: 90 cells.
        • Per cell: draw until 64 teams pass Showdown's validator and are not held-out teams (the 50 meta teams placed 51st to 100th, kept out of every training run and search), at most 1,280 tries. Legal = accepted tries ÷ all tries.
        • Battles: each kept team once against each of the 49 distinct top-50 meta teams, behaviour-cloning policy on both sides, slot order shuffled.
        • Per order: the mean over six asks per seed, then the mean and standard deviation over the three seeds. Against species-first order the seeds are paired; a difference counts when it exceeds twice the larger seed standard deviation.
        • Ask response: the slope of achieved against asked win rate over the four on-grid asks, per seed. A slope of 1 means the model follows the ask exactly.
        • Variety was added after sampling, because the random orders kept only 5 to 17 teams per cell and counts such as effective species grow with the number of teams. It is measured on 200 random five-team subsets per cell; slot-matched distance and novelty use every kept team. It has no pass rule.
