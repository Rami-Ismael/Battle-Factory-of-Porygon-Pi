Asking for more, getting less

Why my discrete diffusion model wins less when I ask for 0.531 than when I ask for 0.5

Rami Ismael · Draft · 30 October 2026

I built a model that writes Pokémon teams, and I can tell it what win rate to aim for. Ask for teams that win half their battles, and the teams it writes win 45% of the time. Ask for 53%, and they win 40%.

Asking for more got me less. The model isn't confused about Pokémon. It's confused about the number 0.531, because almost none of the win rates it learned from sit anywhere near it. Asks that fall between the steps of its training labels lose 3 to 4 points of win rate to asks right next to them on the steps.

The model writes a team one token at a time

A team in Pokémon VGC (Video Game Championships, the official doubles format) is six Pokémon. Each one has a species, an ability, an item, four moves and a Stat Alignment (its nature, such as Adamant), so a team is 48 choices. Asked for 0.5, six in ten of the teams my model writes use exactly these six Pokémon:

The model is a discrete diffusion model trained from scratch on valid Pokémon teams. It starts with all 48 fields hidden behind a [MASK] token and fills them in one at a time. Every field value is a token, a word from a fixed vocabulary of species, abilities, items, moves and Stat Alignments, and the model reads each one by looking it up in an embedding table.

It also takes a target, the ask: the win rate I want the team to reach. In training, each team came with the win rate it actually reached. When I generate, I hand the model the win rate I want, and classifier-free guidance, a setting that makes the model lean harder on the ask, pushes its choices toward teams that fit. I use a guidance weight of 2.

Win rate: the share of battles a team wins against the top-50 meta teams. It plays one battle against each of the 49 distinct teams on that list, and the one team listed twice counts twice. The same AI player, trained by copying human battles, plays both sides, and the six Pokémon are shuffled every battle. Every ask in this post: 64 teams, three seeds.

I asked for 0.531 instead of 0.5, and the teams won less.

The ask is the only input that isn't a token

Every other input comes from a vocabulary. The ask is a number between 0 and 1, so the model needs a different way to read it. Line 206 of code/src/lossdown.py turns it into 17 numbers:

# w is the ask; the frequencies are π, 2π, 4π, … 128π
freqs = 2 ** arange(8) * π
input = [w, sin(w·freqs), cos(w·freqs)]

Sines and cosines like these are how diffusion models usually read a continuous time step. Each frequency doubles the last, which lets the model tell apart values that sit very close together. The fastest pair, at 128π, repeats every 1/64 of win rate, about 0.016. None of this touches the masking, the unmasking or the training loss. It only decides how the ask gets in.

The win rates it learned from sit on a grid

Most training labels come from scoring a team over 24 battles. A score over 24 battles is always a multiple of 1/24: 12 wins is 0.5, 13 wins is 0.5417, and nothing lands in between. The model averages two networks. The one I checked learned from 12,712 teams labelled with a win rate, and 95% of those labels sit exactly on that grid.

So the model saw plenty of teams labelled 12/24 (493 within 0.005 of it) and 13/24 (344), and almost none near 0.531 (11). The low end is the same: 1,268 teams near 7/24 and 9 near 0.3. Between 12/24 and 13/24, the fastest sine turns through 2.7 full cycles, and almost no labels tell the model what any of those values should mean.

Asks between the steps lose 3 to 4 points

To test this, I asked for the win rates on the grid right next to the two odd asks and kept everything else the same: the same model, 64 teams per seed, the same three seeds and the same battles.

Ask 0.531 lost to 13/24 in every seed, by 0.036 to 0.045. Ask 0.3 even lost to the lower ask 7/24, in every seed. On the grid, the model follows the ask up to 0.5 and then levels off near 0.445. Off the grid, it falls below that line.

Round the ask, then fix the encoding

The cheapest fix costs nothing: round every ask to the nearest 1/24 before generating. Asking for 0.531 becomes asking for 13/24, which won 0.444 here.

The lasting fix matches how the model reads everything else. Make the ask a token too, with one embedding per 1/24 step, looked up like a species or a move. Then there is nothing between the steps to misread. Jittering each training label by up to 1/48, or keeping only the slow frequencies, would also smooth it out. I haven't tested any of the training fixes yet.

What I take from it: a number you condition on is only as continuous as the labels it learned from. Mine learned almost entirely from 25 values and was asked for the gaps between them.

Going deeper

How I found this

I was testing remasking (Wang, Schiff et al., 2025, "Remasking Discrete Diffusion Models with Inference-Time Scaling"), a sampler change that lets the model take back its least confident choices and redraw them. I hoped it would close the gap between the win rate I ask for and the one I get. It raised win rate at every ask, but by pulling teams toward the single most common team, and it halved their variety without tracking the ask at all. Remasking random fields instead matched plain decoding. While checking the plain decoder's numbers, the dip at 0.531 stood out, and the label grid explained it.

Is it a bug in the code?

No bug turned up. An earlier audit of the training code and a fresh review by a second AI model both found that the ask reaches the network the same way in training and in generation. The cause is a design choice, a very sharp encoding of one number, meeting labels that almost all take one of 25 values.

What else I ruled out

The old training scores were battled with each team's Pokémon in a fixed order, and the battle AI favours some slots over others. So I re-battled these teams in a fixed order (alphabetical, as the matchup database stores them) instead of shuffled. Average scores moved by less than 0.02, so slot order doesn't explain the dip.
