Loss-Down Retrain

The diffusion model that writes VGC teams now predicts teams it has never seen with 40% less surprise. This page shows where that 40% came from on one real team, then asks the question the loss cannot answer: do its teams win more?

What the loss counts

A team is 48 fields: six Pokémon, each with a species, an ability, an item, four moves and a nature. To score a team, the model reveals it one field at a time in a fixed order (all six species, then abilities and items, then moves, then natures). Before each reveal it says how likely every value is. If it gave the true value probability p, it pays −ln p nats of surprise. Certainty costs 0, a coin flip 0.69, a one-in-a-hundred guess 4.6.

The loss is the average surprise per field over 500 teams the model never trained on. Below is one of them, a Sand team the search loops wrote in September. Switch models to see where the surprise sits, and click any field for its numbers.

Three things stand out. Abilities go from expensive to nearly free as soon as impossible values are ruled out: once Gallade is on the page, only two abilities are legal. The species row barely moves under any model, because picking six Pokémon is the genuinely open part of team building. And the single most surprising field for every model is Muddy Water on Tyranitar, a legal but unusual move; the new model’s best guess there was Protect.

Why the order matters

A legality mask only helps when the field it depends on is already visible. Gallade’s ability depends on its species, so if the species is revealed first, the ability question has two possible answers. The old model was trained to fill fields in random order, so it often faced the ability before anyone knew the species. Figure 2 counts the legal options at the moment each field is predicted, averaged over the 500 test teams.

Masked-diffusion papers give the training-side version of this: let different fields unmask at different times, so the model learns the order it will be used in (Shi et al., NeurIPS 2024; Garg et al., 2025). Species first, then ability and item, then moves, then nature is also the order every search loop already decodes in. Making training match it was worth about 0.04 nats per field on top of the masks.

The other ~120k teams in the logs (the temperature campaign, cem and reverse-score runs, random fills, HPS) never lowered the loss on these test teams, whether mixed in directly or used for pretraining. They come from different generators at different temperatures, so they teach a different distribution.

Who a team beats

The matchup matrix stores each team’s result against every top-50 meta team. Is that row informative about the team itself, beyond its overall win rate? A model was trained to read the row, and 1,000 matrix teams it never saw were scored three ways on the same masks: with nothing, with the win rate, and with the win rate plus the row.

Does a better fit write better teams?

Why battle at all: the loss only measures how well the model predicts teams the search loops already wrote, and most of those teams lose three battles in four (the 500 test teams have a median win rate of –). A model can get much better at predicting weak teams without getting better at writing strong ones. The only measure of the second is to battle what it writes.

The protocol copies the asked-vs-got experiment of 2026-10-04 exactly, so the old model’s numbers compare cell for cell: 64 Showdown-valid teams per cell, either unconditioned or asked for a win rate of 0.3, 0.5 or 0.7 at guidance 1, 2 or 4; one battle against each top-50 team; the VGC-Bench behaviour-cloning policy on both sides; corpus Stat Point spreads. A top-50 team scores 0.531 against the other 49.

Exact loss vs ELBO. Training minimizes the ELBO, a bound averaged over random reveal orders. The numbers here are the exact loss of the fixed order the generator actually samples in (DUEL, ICML 2026), which is lower and needs no bound. Both fall by about 40%; the write-up has both.

Test set. The 500 held-out teams from asked_vs_got’s split, conditioned on their win-rate labels; every copy of them was removed from all training data.
