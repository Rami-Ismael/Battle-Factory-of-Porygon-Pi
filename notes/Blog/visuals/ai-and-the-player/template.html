    Decide

    Kellin Pelrine beat JBXKata005, the strongest playable bot on at the time, in 14 of 15 games. Does that make humans the stronger players again?

    He used a learned , a blind spot in that bot. The same researcher also beat at 100,000 with network b18c384nbt-uec after studying the adversary's games. Both are historical cases against named systems, not a 2026 ranking of the best human against the best AI.

    The project reports no live algorithmic help in these human games. It does not state the hardware or every time and rule setting.

    What would prove a general reversal?

    A dated, repeatable evaluation against a named current engine, with its version, hardware, search budget, rules, time limits, enough games and an uncertainty estimate. One exploit against one checkpoint cannot settle it. No such reversal was found.

    3 · Top left

    Does it teach? Yes, in the opening

    Two studies of games find engine-rated move quality rising once strong AI was available. Both look only at the , the start of the game.

    Which moves each study measured

    Game length beyond move 120 is cut off on purpose. None of these windows reaches the middle or end of a game.

    Shin et al. · 2023

    5.8 million professional decisions, 1950–2021. Decision quality and sequence novelty rise after the AI transition.

    Choi et al. · 2025 manuscript

    749,190 moves by 1,241 professionals, 2015–2019. Gains are larger for younger players.

    Abhishek Nagaraj's reply to Brown points at that age effect. A separate survey of Go teachers reports both satisfaction and worry about AI tools, which is about teaching, not competitors.

    Why this is evidence of change, not proof of cause

    Shin compares years with player fixed effects and robustness checks; nobody was randomly given an engine. Choi uses time trends and age groups, has no data on who used an engine, and the older group may have used one too.

    4 · Top right

    Do players understand more, or remember more?

    A competitive player writing as TuesdayBornWhale disputes what the learning numbers mean.

    The researchers tested

    Moves that deviate from the AI, unfamiliar sequences, later opening moves, and simulated AI sequences. Improvement survives.

    The player objects

    A brand-new whole board can still be built from memorised . Those tests would not catch that.

    Then the PNAS Nexus study adds a twist: new opening patterns arrived, and professionals converged on a few of them.

    Guess, then reveal · top skill decile of professionals

    In 2003, the ten patterns that would dominate 2021 made up 4% of what top professionals played. Each point is 1%. Drag to guess their share in 2021.

    4%

    Reported values: top decile 4.02% → 66.8%, bottom decile 2.48% → 52.6% (Figure 5 discussion, p. 4). These are the ten patterns prominent in 2021 traced back to 2003, not each year's own top ten. Points are rounded to whole percents.

    Novelty and variety can move in opposite directions. Shin's novelty asks whether a game departs from every recorded sequence. Choi's diversity asks how spread out play is across eight-move . Both can be true at once. Neither measures enjoyment.

    Can win probability mislead?

    The critique notes that the same point loss moves the estimated win chance by different amounts depending on the position and . That limits comparisons across stages of a game. It does not by itself undo comparisons of similar positions.

    5 · Bottom left

    Is play fair? It depends when the AI shows up

    The same engine plays four different roles in a player's day. Move it along the track. Every stone in this corner is white: rules, accounts and a synthesis, no measurements.

    AI

    Players on the forum describe suspected engine help and what it does to trust. That is a reported concern, not a measured rate of cheating. Agreement with an engine also happens in honest play, so it cannot convict anyone.

    6 · Bottom right

    Is it enjoyable? Nobody measured it

    Your prediction

    Black stones in this corner: none.

    The one white stone here is David Bessis's essay on mathematics. He argues that understanding and meaning are worth something apart from output, and he explicitly warns against treating mathematics like a fixed-rule game such as Go. It frames the question. It is not data about Go.

    The marked point is the finding: none of the inspected sources measures how online competitors feel, or whether they keep playing. That is a gap, not proof that nothing was lost.

    The dashed rings show what would fill the corner:

    • Repeated satisfaction and agency surveys of competitive online players
    • Platform retention data, with clear periods and comparison groups
    • Records of engine access and review habits
    • Independently assessed live assistance
    • Learning measured separately from all of the above

    Check your reading

    A player's moves agree with the engine more often than last year. What can you conclude about their enjoyment?

    They might be learning with enthusiasm, memorising under pressure, or handing decisions to the engine. Enjoyment needs its own measurement.

    7 · The whole board

    What the board lets you say

    You can sayAI changed how professionals play the opening: engine-rated quality rose, new patterns spread, play concentrated.
    Say with a qualifierThat this is learning. It is engine agreement in the opening, and a player disputes whether it is understanding.
    You cannot say from this evidenceThat AI made online Go less enjoyable or drove players away, or that humans are stronger than AI again.

    The useful question is narrower than the hypothesis: which uses of AI make the player stronger, and which take away the decisions that make competing worth doing?

    Carrying this to Pokémon VGC

    Go suggests keeping study, practice, live help and review apart, and measuring skill and enjoyment separately. It does not predict VGC: VGC has hidden information, randomness, team preparation and rules that change each regulation.

    The draft introduction from the Codex brief

    Go offers a useful test of life after superhuman AI. Research reports changes in professional play and improved engine-evaluated opening decisions, but players dispute how much of that reflects deeper understanding. Online, the question also depends on how the tool is used: studying with an engine differs from receiving undisclosed help during a match. The evidence here leaves open whether AI reduces enjoyment or participation. It points instead to a question worth investigating: when does AI help people become better players, and when does it take away the decisions that make competition rewarding?
