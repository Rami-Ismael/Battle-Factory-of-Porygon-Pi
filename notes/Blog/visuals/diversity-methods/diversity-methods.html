Keeping Teams Diverse

A loop that retrains on its own winners ends up proposing the same six Pokémon. Five fixes ran for six generations on three seeds. The fixes that keep the variety lose win rate, and the one that gains win rate adds no variety.

Date2026-10-09

At generation 6, a taboo list keeps the proposals spread over about 437 different compositions (sets of six species, order ignored). Figure 1 shows how far the plain loop falls without one. The price is paid in win rate: wins ÷ battles against the 50 top-placing meta teams, one battle per opponent, with the same behaviour-cloned battle policy on both sides. A typical top-50 team scores 0.531 against the other 49. The taboo list’s battled teams score 0.055 lower than the plain loop’s (95% interval 0.042 to 0.068). Figure 3 sets every fix against the same rule.

The loop proposes one team

The loop runs in generations. In each one, the version-3 diffusion model proposes 512 teams; each is a proposal. A fast win-rate predictor, the surrogate, picks the 128 proposals it rates highest, and those 128 are battled. The battled teams that score 0.531 or more become the elites, the teams the model is fine-tuned on before the next generation. Generation 0 is the model before any fine-tuning, and every variant of the loop starts from it.

The elites also include the 49 top-50 meta teams as fixed anchors. If fewer than 64 teams reach the cut, the 64 best are used.

A composition is the six species of a team. Two teams with the same six species but different moves, items or Stat Points have the same composition. Different compositions counts how many compositions occur among the first 512 valid proposals of a generation, before any ranking. The most it can be is 512.

Exact teams never repeat. All 88,320 proposals measured on 2026-09-08 were different teams down to moves and Stat Points, while compositions fell from 478 to 172 per 512. That is why this page counts compositions.

The plain loop adds nothing to protect variety. It proposes, battles and retrains on its own winners six times. Before looking, place your estimate.

One composition takes over: Whimsicott, Chandelure, Kingambit, Floette-Eternal, Basculegion and Garchomp. This page calls it the dominant six and draws it in orange wherever it appears. Figure 1 opens the plain loop’s proposals the way the game opens a PC box.

Why it collapses

The dominant six does not start on top. In generation 0 the most common composition is a Charizard version of it (Basculegion, Charizard, Floette-Eternal, Garchomp, Kingambit and Whimsicott), with 166 of the 512 proposals. The dominant six has 81. None of the Charizard version’s copies was among the 128 battled teams, while 40 copies of the dominant six were, and 26 of those reached 0.531. The elites therefore held copies of the dominant six and none of the Charizard version.

From then on the composition that won gets a head start at every stage. In generation 1 the model proposed the dominant six 232 times. The surrogate put 109 of them among the 128 battled, and 79 of the 91 battled teams that reached 0.531 were this six. Fine-tuned on those elites, the model proposed it 413 times in generation 2 and 441 times in generation 3. Figure 1’s readout gives the same three counts for whichever generation is open; click a Charizard slot in generation 0 to see its zero.

The dominant six is a strong team. An earlier loop’s best team has this composition and scored 0.667 on 192 fresh battles.

Five fixes

Each fix is an arm: one variant of the loop, run for six generations on three seeds from the same generation 0. The fixes act at three different stages: proposing, choosing what to battle, and training.

A composition proposed in any earlier generation is rejected, and no composition is accepted more than twice in one generation. The model is unchanged. Sampling stops when 512 proposals pass or after about 4,100 attempts; in 8 of 18 seed-generations fewer passed (as few as 356), so the taboo list’s counts are out of fewer than 512.

Fewer proposals leave fewer chances to find a new composition, so the taboo list’s counts of different compositions are conservative. With 512 proposals in every generation they could only be higher.

The pass rule was fixed before any battle. An arm passes if, at generation 6, it has more different compositions than the plain loop in every seed, at least 176 (generation 0’s count), a smaller largest-composition share (the fraction of the proposals taken by the most common composition) in every seed, and a win rate no more than 0.03 below the plain loop’s at the 95% bound.

The taboo list

VGC’s Species Clause stops a team from carrying two of the same Pokémon. The taboo list applies a similar rule to whole teams and adds a memory: like the Pokédex marking a species as seen, it records every composition the loop has proposed and refuses it in every later generation. Within one generation it accepts at most two copies of a composition.

In generation 1 the taboo arm drew 3,110 proposals on average to pass 512, and rejected 2,598 of them (three-seed means). Before the filter, the first 512 proposals held about 122 compositions; after it, the 512 accepted held about 428. Every copy of the dominant six was rejected in every generation after 0, in every seed; generation 0 is shared and unfiltered. Figure 2 runs the two loops side by side.

What variety costs

Figure 3 scores every arm at generation 6 against the pass rule: variety on the left, win rate on the right.

Every fix that adds compositions loses win rate. The one arm that gains win rate, the big pool, has fewer compositions than the plain loop on average. DPP battles a varied 128 each generation: in seed 1, 122 to 128 different compositions among its 128 battled teams. Its proposals stay near generation 0’s level: 123 to 185 different compositions among 512.

What variety bought

A lower average can still hold a wider set of good teams. Call a battled team a winning team if its win rate is at least 0.531, the score of a typical top-50 team. Figure 4 collects every winning team from generations 1 to 6 of seed 1 and groups them by composition.

Verdict

No arm passes. Two fixes keep the variety: the taboo list, at about 437 different compositions at generation 6, and peeling, at about 417. Only the taboo list keeps many strong teams: about 254 compositions per seed with a win rate of at least 0.531, against peeling’s about 30, and about 78 of the taboo list’s share at most three species with the dominant six. The taboo list pays 0.055 of win rate; peeling pays 0.207.

DPP comes closest on win rate, 0.030 below the plain loop, but stays at about 174 compositions. Islands lose too much win rate (0.081) and stay below generation 0’s variety. The big pool gains win rate (+0.055) and adds no variety. The next experiment combines the two partial successes, the taboo list on proposals with DPP choosing what to battle, and a longer run will show whether the taboo list holds its variety past generation 6.
