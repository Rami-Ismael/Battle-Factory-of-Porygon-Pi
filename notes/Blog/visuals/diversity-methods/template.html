Keeping Rosters Diverse

A loop that trains on its own best teams ends up proposing one team. Five fixes were tried for six generations on three seeds. One keeps the variety. None keeps the win rate.

Data6 arms · 3 seeds · 6 generations
ScoredBattles against the top-50 meta teams
Date2026-10-09

The loop in this project works in generations. The version-3 diffusion model proposes 512 teams, the 128 the surrogate likes best are battled, the winners become the elites, and the model is fine-tuned on the elites before it proposes again. After one generation the loop had climbed from a battled win rate of 0.43 to 0.56. It had also stopped proposing different teams.

To count variety, this page uses one definition. A roster is the six species of a team with order ignored. Two teams with the same six species but different moves are the same roster. Distinct rosters is the number of different rosters among the first 512 valid proposals of a generation, taken before any ranking.

Counting exact teams would hide the problem. Every one of the 88,320 proposals measured on 2026-09-08 was a different team, down to moves and Stat Points, while the number of rosters fell from 478 to 172 per 512. The measures come from the note Metrics for Diversity.
The plain loop runs six generations with nothing to protect variety. How many of its 512 proposals in generation 6 are different rosters? 512 would mean every proposal is its own roster.

What variety costs

The pre-registered rule was strict. An arm passes if, at generation 6, it has more distinct rosters than the plain loop in every seed, at least 176, a smaller largest roster in every seed, and a win rate no more than 0.03 below the plain loop (95% bound). Why the plain loop collapses

Each generation has four stages. Elites train the model, the model proposes 512 teams, 128 of them are battled, and the winners become the next elites. The roster that wins once gets a head start at every stage, so it grows.

Taboo list: sample longer, keep only new rosters

The taboo list leaves the model alone. It keeps sampling until it has 512 proposals that pass the rule: no roster from an earlier generation, and no roster accepted more than twice in this one. The attempts that fail the rule are thrown away.

Peeling: ban the winning core

Peeling masks species out of the sampler. Compare what each loop's proposals are made of: Figure 7 ranks the species that appear in the most proposals.

Islands: four loops that cannot copy each other

Each island is pinned to a different meta team and trains only on its own elites. They are shown here with their 128 proposals each.

DPP: choose the 128 so they are not copies of each other

A determinantal point process picks a set where similar teams count against each other. Picking is greedy: the first team is the best one, and each next pick is the best remaining team after its gain is reduced by how much it overlaps with the teams already chosen.

The verdict

No arm passes. The taboo list is the only fix that keeps real variety: 437 distinct rosters at generation 6 against 38, with a best team that is nearly as good (0.655 to 0.73 against 0.70 to 0.735). It pays 0.055 of average win rate, and it finds about 254 distinct rosters that beat 0.531, of which about 78 sit outside the six-species core the plain loop converges on.

DPP comes closest on win rate, 0.030 below the plain loop. Its battled set is varied but the model still narrows, so it reaches only 174 rosters. Peeling and islands lose too much: banned species and walled-off loops give variety by construction, not strength. The big pool alone changes nothing about variety and gives the highest win rate, 0.605.

The next experiment worth running combines the two partial successes: a taboo list on the proposals, with DPP choosing which proposals to battle. A longer run would also show whether the taboo list keeps its variety past generation 6 or only delays the collapse.
