A search problem has three kinds of properties. Some describe the space before anything is scored, some describe how a score can be observed, and some describe how the score changes as you move. The table summarises all thirteen; the rest of the page takes them in order, one figure at a time.

1 · Search space

What a team is, before any win rate.

Most random teams are illegal

Every benchmark on the list accepts any input: flip any of the 25 bits of contamination control and you still have a valid query. A team does not work that way. Each Pokémon's ability, item and moves must come from what that species can legally have, and two clauses act across the whole team: no two Pokémon of the same species, no two holding the same item.

Fill every field of a team at random, 2,000 times. How many come out legal? Drag to place your guess.
1,000

Known and cheap. Unlike the one collected benchmark with black-box constraints (welded beam), a team's legality is checked by a fast, exact validator. The problem is the encoding, not the check.

The space is huge, and most of the size is padding

The largest combinatorial benchmark in the set has about 1018 points. A VGC team has 84 fields and up to 10112 combinations. Two adjustments shrink that, and the reader can apply both.

Neighbours. A one-bit benchmark has 24–100 single-edit neighbours. A VGC team has 108.9 without replacing a whole Pokémon and 1019.9 with it, so no local search can try every edit.

2 · Evaluation

How f can be observed.

Noise is a dial you set, and it costs time

Ten of the eleven benchmark implementations measured here return the same number every time you ask. A team's win rate is an average of battles, each one a coin flip weighted by the team's strength, so every score comes with binomial noise of variance p(1 − p)/n. The experimenter chooses n, and pays for it in seconds.

No known optimum. MaxSAT-60, LABS-50 and Ackley-53 have known best values, so papers report regret. VGC has none and no bound below 1.0. What it has instead is a bar: the best human-built team, which the search has not beaten.
Stationary, for now. Ranking 176 teams against the June meta teams or the August ones agrees at Spearman 0.904; two random halves of the same opponents agree at 0.908. Within one regulation, no measurable drift.

3 · Landscape

The shape of f over the space, given an edit operator.

One edit, six kinds

Smoothness depends on what counts as one step. For a bit string there is one kind of step. For a team there are six, and they behave very differently. The statistic ρ asks how much one edit moves f compared with jumping to an unrelated team, after removing battle noise: 1 is perfectly smooth, 0 is no better than a random jump.

Which field matters depends on the team. Among search-found teams, the ranking of edit types reproduces at the same team (0.28) and shifts between teams (gap −0.20, interval below zero). At real meta teams battle noise hides it. Every benchmark shows this shift too.

Pokémon work in pairs, and that is about it

Take four edits around a team and try all 16 on/off combinations. The variation splits into what each edit does alone, what pairs do together, and what three or four do together.

A hill climb that can barely take a step

One way to count local optima is to run many hill climbs and count where they stop. The same climb ran on every landscape: propose a random single edit, accept it only if it is better, stop after 40 rejections in a row.
