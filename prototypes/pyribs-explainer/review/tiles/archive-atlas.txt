Every quality diversity run in pyribs is a map. Its axes are the measures you choose, each stored solution is a lit spot on it, and the brightness is the objective. This page follows one stream of candidates onto that map and shows which ones each admission rule keeps.

WHAT RIBS MEANS
Rapid Illumination of Behavior Space

RIBS is the design pyribs implements: an archive that stores solutions, one or more emitters that propose new ones, and a scheduler that connects them. Your own code evaluates each candidate between the scheduler's ask and tell.

The name comes from the title of Fontaine and coauthors, "Covariance Matrix Adaptation for the Rapid Illumination of Behavior Space". Pyribs is Python plus RIBS.

Illumination means finding good solutions all over measure space, not one global best. Behavior space is the older name for measure space. Measures were once called behavior characteristics or behavior descriptors.

The map on the right is that space. An emitter throws each candidate from a stored solution. The archive decides whether it stays. The scheduler carries the verdict back to the emitter.

Pick a rule and the same stream gets judged again. Step through to see the reason for each verdict: the k neighbour links, a dashed ring at the novelty threshold, and a solid ring at the candidate's mean neighbour distance. When the solid ring is wider than the dashed one, the candidate counts as novel.

THE THREE PARTS
Archive, emitter and scheduler
Archive

Pyribs defines it as a data structure that stores the solutions the search generates, together with each solution's objective, measures and other fields. Its add method returns an add_info dictionary. For GridArchive, status 0 means not added, 1 means it beat the cell's incumbent, 2 means it found a new cell.

The grid is one geometry among several. CVTArchive splits space into Voronoi regions around fixed centroids. ProximityArchive has no cells at all and admits by distance to stored points.

Emitter

Pyribs calls emitters "algorithms that generate solutions and adapt to objective, measure, and archive insertion feedback." ask() returns a batch of candidates. tell(...) receives them back with their evaluations and add_info. GaussianEmitter samples stored parents and adds Gaussian noise. EvolutionStrategyEmitter runs an evolution strategy and adapts it from a ranking of the feedback.

Scheduler

Pyribs calls it "A basic class that composes an archive with multiple emitters." Scheduler.ask() asks every emitter and joins their batches. Scheduler.tell(objective, measures) adds the batch to the archive, cuts the results back into each emitter's share, and calls each emitter's tell.

LEVEL OF ABSTRACTION
Where your code touches each one

In the ordinary loop your code calls only the scheduler. You build the archive and the emitters once, then read the archive for results. You touch an emitter's internals only when you write your own.

The bar under each part shows how often the ordinary loop calls it from your code: every iteration, at setup and readout, or at setup only.

Two exceptions sit lower down. A run with differentiable objectives also calls ask_dqd() and tell_dqd(objective, measures, jacobian) first. Code that imports solutions it already evaluated, or runs its own control flow, can call archive.add directly.

WHY A SCHEDULER
Several throwers, one map, one verdict each

With more than one emitter, someone has to join their batches, send one batch through your evaluation, and return each emitter only the verdicts on its own candidates. The scheduler does that bookkeeping.

The pyribs paper gives the scheduler the job of managing how archive and emitters interact. Running several emitters lets the search explore several regions at once. Evaluation stays outside because its dependencies and hardware differ from one application to the next.

BanditScheduler goes further and decides where effort goes. It uses Upper Confidence Bound 1 to pick num_active emitters from a pool, fed by insertion success. A third scheduler, BayesianOptimizationScheduler, pairs with BayesianOptimizationEmitter.

The pyribs docs do not say outright that the scheduler exists to save you this join and slice code. That reason is inferred from the source.

NOVELTY SEARCH IN PYRIBS
Three admission rules on one ProximityArchive

All three use ProximityArchive, an archive with no cells. A candidate's novelty is its mean Euclidean distance to its k nearest stored neighbours. The rules differ in what happens next. Switch the map above to each rule to watch them disagree.

ρ(x) = (1/k_eff) · Σ dist(x, μ_i), k_eff = min(k_neighbors, archive size), novel if ρ(x) ≥ novelty_threshold
Plain novelty search

local_competition=False. Admit if novel, otherwise reject. Objective values are stored but never decide admission. Nothing is replaced. add_info holds status, 0 or 2, and novelty.

Emitter side: EvolutionStrategyEmitter(ranker="nov") ranks candidates by novelty. GaussianEmitter and IsoLineEmitter also work.

Novelty Search with Local Competition

local_competition=True, objectives required. A novel candidate is added whatever its objective. A non-novel one replaces its single closest neighbour only if its objective is strictly higher. status 1 marks a replacement.

LC(x) = Σ 1[ f(x) > f(μ_i) ]

The local competition count is returned but does not decide replacement. ranker="nslc" sorts by Pareto fronts over novelty and that count.

Novelty with threshold decay

Set threshold_decay_rate. After threshold_decay_itrs add calls in a row with no novel insert, the threshold becomes max(threshold_decay_min, threshold * rate) and the counter resets. Any novel insert resets it too. Replacements alone do not.

Decay only lowers the cutoff. The distance formula stays the same. The supported algorithms page has no recipe by this name. It is an archive setting, added in v0.12.0.

TUTORIAL ONE
Orchestrating LLMs to Write Diverse Stories with Quality Diversity through AI Feedback

Each solution is a story string. A 20 by 20 GridArchive over romance and happy ending, both rated 1 to 10, keeps the best story in each of its 400 cells. After 200 iterations, 28.5% of the cells are lit.

One large language model, Llama 3.1 8B instruct at q4_K_M, does both jobs. It rewrites stories, and it rates every candidate five times each for quality, romance and happy ending. That makes 15 rating calls per story, each clipped to [1, 10] and averaged.

The emitter is a custom LLMDirectionalEmitter, a subclass of EmitterBase. With an empty archive it returns three seed stories. After that it samples one stored story and picks up or down for each measure, for example "make the story sound more like a romance story" and "make the ending of the story less happy". The four diagonal arrows in the map are those four choices. It defines no tell, so it never adapts.

A plain Scheduler(archive, emitters) runs 200 iterations, one new story each. The saved run took 35:21.

TUTORIAL TWO
Incorporating Human Feedback into Quality Diversity for Diversified Text-to-Image Generation

Here the map's axes are learned, and they are relearned at iterations 21, 51 and 101. Each time, every stored elite is scored again, gets new coordinates, and is put into a fresh 20 by 20 archive. Coverage drops at each of those iterations, then climbs again.

A solution is a Stable Diffusion latent of 4 by 32 by 32, 4096 numbers, decoded to a 256 by 256 image for "an astronaut riding a horse on mars". The objective is a rescaled CLIP score, computed once per image.

The measures come from DivProj, one linear layer from 512 CLIP image features to two coordinates. It is trained on triplets labelled by DreamSim, which stands in for a human. Each retrain adds 1000 triplets and fits a fresh model. Grid bounds are the 1st and 99th percentiles of the new coordinates.

The emitter is a new GaussianEmitter with sigma=0.1 and batch 4. It inherits a tell that does nothing. The saved run of 200 iterations took 14:21.

The axes have no names. The notebook's collage shows smaller objects toward the upper left and a darker sky toward the top, trends seen after the fact.
