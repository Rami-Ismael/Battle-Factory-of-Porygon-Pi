# Uniform noise drives repeated token changes

| One position's illustrative trajectory | Total value changes | Changes beyond the first | What the count establishes |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| `[MASK] → A → A → A` | 1 | 0 | One irreversible reveal |
| `Q → R → A → A` | 2 | 1 | Repeated changes, potentially before any meaningful prediction |
| `Q → A → B → A` | 3 | 2 | A departure and return, possible with an unchanged prediction of `A` |

**Uniform-noise samplers can change the same position repeatedly, and actual published trajectories demonstrate that they do.** The strongest direct evidence in the original papers is a researcher count of three character-level examples, each with at least two observed changes at 177 of 256 positions. However, these papers do not establish a general frequency distribution across models or a measured split between noise-driven edits and changed predictions. A reverse sampler can leave a value that the model predicts with certainty, so repeated sampled-value changes do not require the model to change its mind. Changes are concentrated early in the displayed character examples and in a controlled fixed-prediction calculation, but late changes remain possible, and newer greedy-refinement experiments show substantial late instability. Guidance can raise or lower the change probability depending on the conditional and unconditional distributions. **The project's proposed count is defensible as an operational measure of repeated state changes; it primarily measures sampling-path activity, including noise-to-noise movement, rather than demonstrated error correction.** ([Published character trajectories, Figure 13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03006v3#page=31); [uniform reverse posterior, Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1); [late refinement experiment, Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.01275v1))

## Repeated changes are established, but their general rate remains unmeasured

Discrete diffusion generates a sequence by repeatedly updating categorical values at fixed positions. Training first corrupts clean examples, then teaches a model to predict how to undo the corruption. With uniform noise, corruption substitutes random vocabulary values, including values that coincidentally equal the original. Generation starts with random values already occupying every position. With absorbing noise, corruption sends values to a special `[MASK]` state. The ordinary reverse sampler reveals a value and then preserves it. The comparison here assumes that every generated position is eventually revealed. Remasking and correction extensions remove that irreversibility. ([Schiff et al., Sections 2.1 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.10193v3); [implemented reverse transitions](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1211-L1253))

## A certain, unchanged predictor still produces revisions

The model's predicted clean token and the sampler's current token are different objects. A categorical reverse transition combines the clean prediction with a time-dependent noise process. Thus even certainty about the current value does not generally force the next sampled value to equal it. The derivation below concerns **Schiff's finite-step plug-in posterior**, not every sampler used by the other papers. ([Appendix A, Equation 25](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1); [posterior implementation](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L356-L381))

**Derivation from that posterior:** let the current value be `i`, the vocabulary size be `N`, and the predicted probability of clean value `i` be `c`. Let `a = alpha(t)` and `b = alpha(s)` for a reverse step from noisier time `t` to cleaner time `s`, with `b > a`. Here `alpha` is the clean-signal weight. Define `d = b-a` and `D = N*a*c + 1-a`. Summing posterior probabilities over every destination other than `i` gives

```text
P(next value differs from i)
  = d*(1-c)/D + d*(N-1)*(1-b)/(N*b*D)
    model-directed mass        uniform-noise mass
```

This is an exact algebraic separation of probability mass in this parameterization. It is not an observed causal label for each edit. In particular, model-directed mass does not mean that the model changed its prediction since the preceding step. A fixed prediction of `A` still directs a return to `A` after noise moves the current state elsewhere. When `c=1`, the model-directed term vanishes but the noise term remains positive at interior times. The departure probability then becomes

```text
A = (d/b)*(N-1)/N*(1-b)/(1+(N-1)*a).
```

When the model is certain of a different value, `c=0`, the change probability is

```text
B = (d/b)*(1-(1-b)/N)/(1-a).
```

The total change probability decreases as `c` increases. Confidence therefore means probability assigned to the **current token**, not merely a high maximum probability assigned somewhere in the vocabulary. These are deductions from the cited posterior, rather than measurements of trained models. ([Equation 25](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1))

A controlled baseline makes the consequences for the proposed metric explicit. Fix the prediction to one clean target `x` with probability one for the entire run. Initialize from the uniform prior. Use 189 categories, 128 equally spaced reverse steps from `t=1` to `10^-5`, and the ideal schedule `alpha(t)=1-t`. The numbers below are **deterministic posterior calculations**, independently recomputed for this synthesis. No model was trained or sampled.

| Derived quantity in this fixed-predictor experiment | Expected count per position |
| --- | ---: |
| All sampled-value changes | 5.404400 |
| Changes beyond the first departure from initialization, the project's candidate metric | **4.404443** |
| Changes after first reaching the fixed predicted target, excluding the first arrival | **0.095813** |
| Sum of departure probabilities evaluated as if always at the target | 1.444097 |

To reproduce the calculation, the probability of entering `x` from another value is `E = (d/b)*(b+(1-b)/N)/(1-a)`. Let `v` be the probability of currently occupying `x`, initially `1/N`. Each step adds `v*A+(1-v)*B` to the total expected changes and updates `v` to `v*(1-A)+(1-v)*E`. If `u` is the probability of never having visited `x`, initially `1-1/N`, the expected changes after its first visit add `v*A+(1-v-u)*B`, with `u` updated to `u*(1-E)`. All additions use the pre-update probabilities. Finally, expected changes beyond the first departure equal expected total changes minus the probability of any change. That probability is

```text
1 - [(1/N)*product(1-A) + (1-1/N)*product(1-B)].
```

The sum 1.444097 uses `A` at every step while assuming the current token remains the endorsed target. It is **not** the expected revisions for a fixed target, because actual trajectories move away and return. Likewise, the small 0.095813 count begins after the first target visit, whereas the candidate count starts after the first change away from random initialization. Their difference shows that the candidate can count substantial movement before reaching the target, even with no prediction change whatsoever. These results follow from the posterior above and the stated grid. They are an illustrative baseline, not a measured attribution fraction for real generation. ([Source posterior and sampling grid](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1126-L1155); [full supporting derivation in the supplied notes](../research_notes/Uniform%20diffusion%20token%20revisions/posterior_analysis.md))

The idealized schedule matters. The inspected upstream noise class actually gives `alpha(t)=1-0.999*t`, while the calculations use `1-t` exactly. Vocabulary size, step budget, schedule, stopping endpoint, and final decoding policy all affect counts. The supplied calculation also gives 0.027387 post-target changes with schedule `alpha(t)=1-t^16` under the same 189-category, 128-step setup, versus 0.095813 for the linear schedule. These values cannot be transferred to a trained team generator without measuring its trajectory. ([Noise implementation](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/noise_schedule.py#L134-L153); [source posterior](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1))

**No measured noise-versus-prediction-change percentage is established by this source set.** Prediction changes alter the reverse transition, but changes in sampled values alone cannot identify them. Even a stable top prediction can conceal probability redistribution, and a changing top prediction need not cause a sampled edit. A frozen-prediction baseline isolates a useful mechanism, while differing contexts and noise levels prevent treating it as a universal amount to subtract from real runs.

## Changes often concentrate early, while late refinement can remain unstable

**Researcher counts from Figure 13:** summing Austin's three displayed examples gives 731, 361, 243, 129, and 39 changed positions across successive displayed intervals. The first two intervals contain 1,092 of 1,503 observed changes, about 72.7%, over about 40% of the displayed reverse-time range. All 39 changes in the final interval occur at positions that already changed earlier. This supports early concentration and late re-editing in these examples, not a universal time profile. Sparse checkpoints cannot recover hidden excursions or exact settlement times. ([Austin, Figure 13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03006v3#page=31))

**Derived timing under a fixed predictor:** the supplied posterior calculation places 61.294% of post-target changes in the first quarter of the linear-schedule run, and 8.400% in the final quarter. With exponent 16, those shares become 99.451% and approximately zero after rounding. This does not imply that a wrong value cannot change late. Near the clean endpoint, departures from an endorsed token become rare, while the probability of moving away from a token the model rejects can approach one. Changed model predictions can also produce late edits. These are properties of the stated posterior experiment, not settling-time measurements of trained models. ([Posterior](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1); [calculation details](../research_notes/Uniform%20diffusion%20token%20revisions/posterior_analysis.md))

**Author-reported measurement in newer work:** Nafez and Henderson examine 512 samples of length 512 after 500 ancestral steps followed by 20 deterministic greedy updates. At greedy step 20, standard Duo, UDLM, and uniform-noise SEDD still change 173–270 positions per step across the models. Their retention regularization reduces changes to a plateau of 3–11 positions within roughly ten steps. These are per-step adjacent-state counts, not cumulative revisions per position. Because this tail uses deterministic maximum-probability updates, its continued edits cannot be attributed to fresh categorical sampling noise. It demonstrates instability of the learned update dynamics in this refinement setting; it does not quantify the ordinary ancestral sampler's noise-versus-model split. ([Correct-token retention study, Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.01275v1))

Other revision-capable samplers reinforce the importance of defining the counted event. **Approximate researcher readings of a graph:** Kollovieh and colleagues count cumulative categorical transitions over 50 steps on molecular graphs. At resampling coefficient one, Figure 6 shows roughly 15 node and 12.5 edge changes with time-grid exponent one, versus roughly 6.5 and 5.5 with exponent four. This closely resembles counting actual state changes, but concerns graph categories and includes no first-setting subtraction. **Author-reported dataset totals:** Xiang and colleagues distinguish revision operations from effective lexical replacements. On the HumanEval code-generation benchmark, their Saber baseline with the Large Language Diffusion with mAsking model, LLaDA-Instruct with eight billion parameters, makes 16,081 revision operations but only 144 effective replacements. Operations can remask a token and restore the same value. Their proposed Cache Override Verification for Efficient Revision, COVER, reports 138 operations and 99 effective replacements in that comparison. Neither operation count is interchangeable with adjacent raw token-ID changes. ([Controllable resampling, Section 4.4 and Figure 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17310v1); [verification definitions and Table 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06161v2))

Prediction stability is a further distinct quantity. The newer mutual-stability sampling study reports that Dream-7B's top-1 prediction flip rate falls to at most 5% in the final quarter of executed trajectories. This concerns a masked model's predictions, including mutable positions, rather than repeated replacements after an irreversible reveal. The self-reflective remasking method RemeDi's reported remasking frequency likewise does not establish cumulative lexical edit multiplicity; the inspected definition does not resolve whether repeated remasks at one position are deduplicated. These results do not establish early settlement for uniform-noise sampling. ([Mutual-stability study, Section 6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.16908v1); [self-reflective remasking, Section 5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.23653v1))

## Guidance changes edit probability in either direction

Classifier-free guidance combines a prediction conditioned on the desired property with an unconditional prediction. In Schiff's uniform-noise implementation, each clean prediction first becomes a reverse posterior. The sampler then geometrically combines those two posteriors. It does not combine clean logits first. Let `q_c` and `q_u` denote the conditional and unconditional reverse posteriors, with guidance strength `gamma`. ([Implemented guidance](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1314-L1328))

**Derivation:** the guided probability of destination `j` is proportional to `q_c(j)^gamma * q_u(j)^(1-gamma)`. Relative to staying at current value `i`, sum the guided odds of all alternatives:

```text
S_gamma = sum over j != i of
          [q_c(j)/q_c(i)]^gamma * [q_u(j)/q_u(i)]^(1-gamma)
P(change under guidance) = S_gamma / (1+S_gamma).
```

For `gamma > 1`, guidance lowers the change probability if conditioning strengthens staying relative to all alternatives; it raises it if conditioning instead strengthens alternatives relative to staying. If the two posteriors agree, guidance has no effect. In particular, two predictions certain of the same current token preserve the unguided noise-driven departure probability. Certainty alone does not eliminate it. ([Guidance equation and posterior construction](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#S3.SS1))

**Numerical derivation, not measurement:** with `alpha(t)=1-t`, `t=0.5`, `s=0.49`, 189 categories, and a uniform unconditional clean prediction, increasing `gamma` from one to two lowers change probability from 0.0001006001 to 0.0000005089 when the conditional prediction is certain of the current value. If it is certain of a different value, the same increase raises change probability from 0.0391140160 to 0.8060495419. These opposite examples rule out a general monotonic rule. The inspected literature supplies no controlled measurement of classifier-free or classifier-based guidance strength against realized uniform-noise revision counts or last-change times. Guidance-quality curves, remasking controls, and resampling-strength sweeps do not answer that question. ([Source guidance implementation](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1314-L1328); [supporting calculation](../research_notes/Uniform%20diffusion%20token%20revisions/posterior_analysis.md))

## The candidate measures repeated state changes, not corrective decisions

**Recommendation:** retain the candidate as a clearly named trajectory measure, “value changes after the first change.” For states `x[0], ..., x[K]` in generation order, including the actual initialization, define

```text
C_i = sum from k=1 to K of 1[x[k,i] != x[k-1,i]]
R_i = max(C_i - 1, 0)
```

Here `C_i` is the total value-change count, and `R_i` is the number beyond the first. A position with three changes contributes two revisions, rather than merely one revised position. Report mean `R_i`, the fraction with `C_i >= 2`, and the distribution of `C_i` separately. A return to an earlier value counts again. Resampling the same value counts zero. For a completed ordinary absorbing run, every generated position has `C_i=1` and `R_i=0`. For uniform noise, the first departure from the random prior receives the one-change allowance. Calling it a first “commitment” would imply something the process does not establish. This is a proposed project convention supported by transition-count precedents, not a standard metric established by the five original papers. ([Absorbing versus uniform implementation](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1211-L1253); [categorical-transition precedent](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17310v1))

The opening examples show the interpretation. `Q → R → A` receives a revision even if `Q` and `R` are random noise and `A` is the model's first useful output. `Q → A → B → A` receives two revisions even with a permanently fixed prediction of `A`. The candidate therefore primarily characterizes **how much the sampled trajectory moves beyond a one-change baseline**. The evidence does not establish that noise contributes most revisions in every trained model. It establishes that noise can produce a large count without any change in prediction, so that interpretation must remain separate from useful self-correction.

Record changes inside the actual update loop at fixed token positions, excluding fixed conditioning and padding. Include the initial random or masked state. Identify final greedy decoding and correction passes separately, then state whether a reported total includes them. Keep the vocabulary, time grid, schedule, endpoint, guidance, temperature, and update budget with the result. A finely recorded discrete sampler trace measures its realized state changes; sparse snapshots only bound that count, and neither records unobserved continuous-time jumps between retained states. The inspected Schiff sampler returns its final state and uses equality for caching, while Duo's apparent intermediate text logging retains completed growing prefixes rather than every inner denoising state. Such outputs cannot reconstruct the proposed metric retrospectively. ([Schiff loop](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/discrete-diffusion-guidance/blob/edb0f8c28b7caeb4ea7a06a2fee8d74ab6da1661/diffusion.py#L1115-L1209); [Duo prefix logging](https://github.com/s-sahoo/duo/blob/7c9b498f5b717de064d6fad7e2509c866e6cb620/trainer_base.py#L799-L837))

For the model-changing-its-mind question, record the clean predictive distribution as well as the sampled state. Count consecutive top-1 prediction changes separately and track the probability assigned to the current state. A useful diagnostic estimates the two posterior mass contributions above at each step, but labels them as expected transition mass, not proven causes of realized corrections. To locate changes, log change events by generation progress and noise level, and define retrospective settlement as the final change index, zero for a never-changing position. A first visit to the endpoint value is insufficient because the trajectory can leave and return. For remasking comparisons, also count changes between successive assigned lexical values, ignoring intervening masks: `A → [MASK] → A` contains raw state transitions but no lexical replacement. These are proposed engineering diagnostics, informed by the distinction between revision operations and effective replacements. ([Verification definitions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06161v2); [posterior mass derivation](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10193v1#A1))

## Conclusion

A high revision count should trigger inspection of where and why the trajectory moves, rather than automatically count as evidence of stronger reasoning or error recovery. A uniform sampler can score highly by moving among noisy values, while a successful absorbing sampler must score zero under the candidate definition. Conversely, suppressing changes can preserve mistakes. Use the count to describe behavior and compare its relationship to final team validity and counter-team performance; evaluate corrective benefit with a separate quality measure.

It incorporates the later notes' positive measurements rather than preserving the earlier notes' broader negative findings. The synthesis independently checks the saved character-state aggregates and the illustrative fixed-predictor counts; it runs no trained model. Paper references are version-pinned, including revisions later than the original submission years. The reviewed material does not establish a universal revision rate, an empirical noise-versus-prediction attribution, or a general settling-time law. Those are measurements for the project's instrumented sampler to produce.
