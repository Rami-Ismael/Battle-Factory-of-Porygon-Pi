Self-play

A player gets strong by practicing against copies of itself. The whole survey is a map of one question: which copy do you practice against?

1 · The perfect sparring partner is you

To learn a two-player game you need an opponent. Humans are slow and computers playing badly teach you nothing. But a copy of yourself is always exactly your level — every time you improve, your opponent improves too. Practice difficulty goes up on its own, like a ladder that builds itself.

2 · The catch: your opponent keeps moving

Both players change every day, so nothing you learned yesterday is guaranteed to work today — the survey calls this non-stationarity. Worst case, training runs in circles: rock learns to beat scissors, then paper beats rock, then scissors beats paper, forever, with no real progress.

Every method below is a different way to stop the circle.

3 · Vanilla self-play: fight only today's you

The simplest recipe: always play against your newest self — the one that is still learning right now, changing a little after every practice session. Fast, and it is also the recipe behind the famous game-playing AIs you are about to see explained in that YouTube video — but those versions bolt on two extra ingredients, so hold that thought until §7. Nothing here requires having read any of those papers; §7 explains everything on its own. On its own, vanilla self-play can forget. If today's you never meets last month's you, tricks that beat last month's you quietly rot.

4 · Fictitious play: fight everyone you've ever been

Fix the forgetting by keeping every past self in a box — each one a photograph, frozen, that never learns anything again — and drawing opponents from all of them equally. Nothing can rot, because everything old still shows up. The cost: the box grows forever, and most of it is weak old versions that waste your practice time.

Careful: this is not "blend all your past selves into one average player." You never build that player. You pull one photograph out of the box and fight it, whole. The "average" is only what the box looks like after a thousand pulls.

5 · Smarter picks from the same box

Two refinements keep the box but change the draw. δ-uniform: only draw from the newest slice of the box — old enough to not forget, new enough to not waste time. Prioritized fictitious self-play: draw most often the past selves that still beat you — practice where you actually lose.

6 · Double oracle: hire a specialist for every weakness

The population-play branch goes further. Keep a small squad of very different players. On each round, ask: "what one new player would beat this squad's current best mix?" — train exactly that player, add it, repeat. The squad only grows when a real weakness is found, so it stays small but hard to exploit from any direction.

7 · The famous success story — decoded, no papers required

You have not read the AlphaGo/AlphaZero papers and you do not need to — this section assumes nothing. When you watch the video, you will meet four names from one research family. Here is the part the video's "self-play!" headline skips: those four programs are four different recipes from the menu above, and the family changed its mind between generations:

Note the middle one. AlphaGo Zero kept a promotion gate — a new brain only replaced the old one after beating it 55 times out of 100. AlphaZero threw that gate away and just used the newest brain. Gates are a real dial, and people turn them both ways.

So why could the last two get away with the simplest recipe on the menu? Two ingredients that have nothing to do with picking a sparring partner — and both are in the video, so you will recognise them:

Ingredient one: it does not trust its own brain to pick a move. Before every move it runs a big lookahead search — it imagines thousands of continuations and counts which move came out best. Then it trains the brain to copy what the search decided. The search is always a bit smarter than the raw brain, so there is always something better to copy. That is a treadmill that runs uphill by itself. Take the search away and vanilla self-play is a much weaker thing.

Ingredient two: Go has no circle. Remember rock-paper-scissors from §2? Go does not work that way. In Go, strength is a ladder — if A beats B and B beats C, then A beats C, almost always. The survey says it plainly: vanilla self-play "is effective in transitive games, but it can lead the agent to cyclic learning patterns in non-transitive games." On a ladder, chasing your newest self is exactly right, because your newest self is genuinely the hardest opponent you have ever had. Fighting old selves would just be slower.

The lesson to import, and the one not to. The video's takeaway will sound like "vanilla self-play works." It does not transfer here. Pokémon is the circle, not the ladder — that is the whole reason this project measures a table of who-beats-whom instead of a single score. And the plan has no lookahead search bolted on top. Strip both ingredients out and vanilla self-play is not the champion recipe; it is the baseline the other recipes on this menu exist to beat.

8 · Why this paper matters here

The survey is a menu, not a winner — it organizes all of these (and more) into one family tree and shows where each got famous: the four Go programs from §7, prioritized fictitious self-play in StarCraft, fictitious-play descendants in poker, and δ-uniform in Honor of Kings. Nobody famous runs one recipe straight, either — the Dota bot in that same lineage played against its newest self 80% of the time and against a prioritized pick 20% of the time. For this project it maps the battle policy arm only: it's the catalogue of ways a battle policy can be trained against itself. It says nothing about which team to build.

Source: Zhang et al., “A Survey on Self-play Methods in Reinforcement Learning,” arXiv:2408.01072.
