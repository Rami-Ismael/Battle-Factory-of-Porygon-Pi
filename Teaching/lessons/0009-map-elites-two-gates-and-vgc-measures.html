1. Your four claims, corrected

Claim 1 — "there are two styles of battle, attack and defense, with balance between." Right intuition, wrong data type. MAP-Elites never receives a style as input. Styles are not declared; they are recovered: you define numeric measurements taken from simulated battles, discretise them into a grid, and the archetype spectrum re-emerges on its own as regions of that grid. The original paper's promise is exactly this: MAP-Elites creates "a map of high-performing solutions at each point in a space defined by dimensions of variation that a user gets to choose" (🟢 Mouret & Clune 2015, abstract). Your job is choosing dimensions whose spread reproduces attack↔defense automatically — see §5.

Claim 2 — "an N×N matrix, one axis how long the battle takes, another you define." Correct structure, correct name: that matrix is the archive. And battle length is not just acceptable, it is literally one of the Hearthstone paper's two measures — "average game length in turns", range 5–15; their other was average hand size, range 1–7 (🟢 Zhang et al., GECCO 2022, §4.2.2). Hand size has no VGC analogue, so the axis you asked me to define is below in §5.

Claim 3 — "you want a diverse set of metas." Sharpen this: diversity alone is worthless — random teams are maximally diverse and terrible. What quality-diversity buys is diversity among candidates that have already passed a quality bar. Every cell holds the strongest team found so far that behaves like its cell. Bonus you get for free: because MAP-Elites explores more of the space, "it also tends to find a better overall solution than state-of-the-art search algorithms" (Mouret & Clune, same abstract) — relevant to your counter-team hunt, where the single best team is the deliverable.

Claim 4 — "the best performing candidate on [a] grid behavior axis will replace the old." This is the one to fix, and the fix is one phrase: a candidate only ever competes inside the single cell its own measurements land it in. Not against the grid. Not against its parent. Against at most one occupant. That is §3.

3. The two gates

  1. Gate 1 — WHERE. The child's measures decide which cell it lands in. This is not a choice and not a strategy — the simulator produced those numbers, and they place the child mechanically, the way a coordinate places a pin in a map.
  2. Gate 2 — WHETHER. The child's objective decides whether it stays there. If Gate 1 dropped it into an empty niche, it stays unconditionally — any objective qualifies. If the niche is occupied, it survives only by beating that one incumbent's objective. Lose, and it is discarded entirely.

The division of labour, verbatim from lesson 0004: "the measures decide where a child competes (which cell it lands in — not a choice, determined by how it behaved); the objective decides whether it wins there." And the consequence that makes quality-diversity work: a mediocre defensive team never has to beat the best aggressive team — only the best defensive team found so far. A child worse than its own parent can still enter the archive, if its mutations carried its behaviour into a weak or empty region. That sentence is the entire mechanism.

4. Watch it run

The toy archive: x-axis mean battle length (turns), y-axis KO spread — how many of your four battlers scored at least one knockout. Cell numbers are win rate vs the meta suite. Breed children until the pattern is boring: admission without comparison (empty niche), replacement (won its cell), or discard (lost its cell) — and watch coverage and the Σ-objectives counter move. That counter is the QD-score reading: fill more cells, raise the occupants, do either.

Recommended default pair: battle length × KO spread. Reasons: (1) both read straight off a battle log with no interpretation; (2) they are roughly decorrelated — beware pairing the aggression ratio with battle length, since fast sweeps are short by definition and the grid collapses onto a diagonal; (3) together they name styles you actually have to counter: fast hypercarry, slow hypercarry, balanced tempo, full stall.

Two cautions. First, measures and the objective are different jobs — do not reuse the same quantity in both roles (DSA-ME uses end-of-game health difference as its objective; putting margin-of-victory on a measure axis would double-count it). Second, choose ranges before running anything, wide enough that the styles you care about land in visibly different cells — the grid is a claim about what variation matters, and you are the one who gets to choose.

Retrieval practice

A child lands in an occupied cell and its objective is lower than the incumbent's. What happens?

Gate 2 compares the child against exactly one occupant — the elite of the cell Gate 1 chose. Losing means discarding the child; nothing else in the archive was ever consulted.

What decides which cell a child competes in?

Measures decide placement (Gate 1), the objective decides survival (Gate 2). The parent only donated genes; the child's own simulated battles produced its coordinates.

DSA-ME's archive axes were…

Average hand size (range 1–7) and average game length in turns (range 5–15), §4.2.2. Win rate itself is never a measure there — even the objective is the smoothed health-difference proxy, range −30…+30.

Why can a child worse than its parent still enter the archive?

Gate 2 has a free pass: an unoccupied niche has no incumbent to beat. This is the mechanism that keeps diversity alive — exploration pays even when the child is bad.
