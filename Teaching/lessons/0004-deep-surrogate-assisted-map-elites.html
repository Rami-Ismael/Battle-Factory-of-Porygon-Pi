Deep Surrogate Assisted MAP-Elites

The one verified-HIGH paper from both 2026-08-19 sweeps. Its idea in one line: run the search on a cheap fitted model, and spend every expensive simulated evaluation on the exact candidates the model is most confidently wrong about.

Why this, now. Your todo list already commits to comparing simulated annealing and Bayesian optimisation against 𝑓, and 𝑓 is expensive: one candidate team costs a batch of simulated battles. DSA-ME (🟢 Zhang, Fontaine, Hoover & Nikolaidis, GECCO 2022, arXiv 2112.03534) is the only published deck-construction paper that solves exactly that cost problem with a surrogate — an estimator of the objective — trained during the search. After this lesson you should be able to (1) draw its two-loop architecture from memory and price each arrow, (2) state what the surrogate predicts (it is not win rate), and (3) name the ablation result that matters most for any estimator of 𝑓 you ever fit.

2. MAP-Elites in five sentences

MAP-Elites is a quality-diversity method: instead of one best solution it maintains an archive — a grid whose axes are "measure" dimensions (here: average hand size, range 1–7, and average game length in turns, range 5–15) — and keeps the best deck found in each cell. Each step: pick an elite from the archive, mutate it — "we replace k cards from a parent elite with k cards chosen uniformly from the cardset. We vary k geometrically" — a swap operator over the subset, the same neighbourhood move annealing on teams would use. The child is evaluated, its measures place it in a cell, and it stays only if it beats that cell's incumbent. The product is a map of diverse strong decks, scored by QD-score — the paper's own definition (§4.2.2, verbatim): "the sum of the objective values of all solutions in the ground-truth archive". Ground-truth matters: the score counts only elites whose objective (range −30…+30) and measures came from the simulator — the surrogate archive's predicted scores never count. Note the kinship with your argmax-vs-top-k item from Lesson 0001's strand: an archive is a structured top-k.

The division of labour, if one sentence sticks: the measures decide where a child competes (which cell it lands in — not a choice, determined by how it behaved); the objective decides whether it wins there. A mediocre control deck never has to beat the best aggro deck — only the best control deck found so far. That is the mechanism that keeps diversity alive: a child worse than its parent can still enter the archive, if its measures land it in a different — weaker or empty — cell.

3. The contribution: the two-loop architecture

The expensive thing is the 200-game evaluation. So they train a deep surrogate — a 3-hidden-layer MLP (128 / 32 / 16) — online, to predict, from the bag-of-cards vector alone, three numbers: the objective and both measures. (It must predict the measures too, or the surrogate archive cannot place candidates in cells.) The loop:

  1. Inner loop (free): run MAP-Elites for ~10⁶ iterations entirely on the surrogate — zero simulated games — producing a surrogate archive of predicted-good, predicted-diverse decks.
  2. Ground (expensive): take candidates from the surrogate archive and evaluate them on the real simulator, 200 games each. Insert results into the real archive.
  3. Retrain: add those labelled decks to the training set, retrain the surrogate, repeat.

The self-correcting part is why it works: the inner search generates precisely the decks the surrogate is most optimistic about, and those are the ones that get ground-truthed next. Systematic errors are hunted down by the search itself.

Because QD-score "combines both the number of cells filled … and the objective value of each occupant", the paper disambiguates with a CCDF plot (their Fig. 2, right): for each objective threshold on the x-axis, the fraction of cells holding an elite above it. That curve is how you tell "many mediocre elites" apart from "fewer but stronger" when two QD-scores tie.

Two corrections to the way this paper is usually summarised (both caught in the 2026-08-19 adversarial deep-read; see the entry in RESOURCES.md): it does not reduce the number of real simulations — it extracts ~2.5× more quality from the same simulation budget; and the surrogate predicts the smoothed health-difference proxy plus the archive measures, not win rate.

The ablation that matters most for you: Offline DSA-ME — identical, except the surrogate trains on random decks instead of search-generated diverse ones — collapses from 338.5 to 194.9 QD-score. Any estimator of 𝑓 you ever fit will face the same fact: what you train it on matters as much as what you train.

5. What does not transfer

  • Encoding. Bag-of-cards has no per-slot configuration — a card is a card, while your team member bundles species, ability, item, moves, Stat Points, alignment. The authors flag their own encoding as weak: it "does not capture a card's mana cost, attack, health, or card text".
  • Measures. Hand size and game length are Hearthstone-specific and gameplay-derived — a VGC adaptation must invent its own measures, and the surrogate must predict them too.
  • Opponent weights. Six decks weighted evenly ≠ a frequency-weighted meta-team list. The fix looks mechanical (weight the games per opponent), but it is unpublished — nobody has done it.

Retrieval practice

What does DSA-ME's surrogate predict from the deck encoding?

Health difference is the smoothed objective; the two measures (hand size, game length) must be predicted too, or the surrogate archive cannot place candidates in cells.

At the same real-simulation budget, DSA-ME versus plain MAP-Elites is…

338.52 vs 136.89 QD-score at an identical 10,000-evaluation budget — quality per simulation, not fewer simulations.

Why does Offline DSA-ME (surrogate trained on random decks) fall to 194.9?

The surrogate is only accurate where it has labels; the online loop labels exactly the region the search is exploiting, the offline one never does.

Which piece of 𝑓 is missing from DSA-ME's setup?

Their six-deck suite splits games evenly; your meta weights each meta team by how often it appears in the collected list. That combination — surrogate search against a frequency-weighted opponent list — is unpublished.
