Coupling — the marginals are fixed, the joint is yours

The prerequisite nobody teaches: two random quantities can each keep their own distribution while how they move together is entirely your engineering decision.

Lesson 0007 · 2026-08-20 · ~7 minutes · prerequisite for lesson 0006 (CRN) · mission · requested in chat 2026-08-20 (gap found in 0006 §1) · reference: CRN estimator card

Why this, now. Lesson 0006's opening paragraph — "run both sides on the same random draws and the luck cancels" — silently assumed you already saw randomness as something you allocate, not something that just happens. This lesson installs that idea. After it you should be able to (1) say what a marginal and a joint distribution are and which one a coupling changes, (2) build the shared-draw coupling by hand, and (3) explain why the estimate of the difference stays unbiased no matter how the draws are shared.

1. Two words: marginal, joint

Take one paired battle: X = 1 if team T₁ wins its battle, Y = 1 if team T₂ wins its battle. Two vocabulary items describe the pair (X, Y):

wordwhat it answershere
marginal of Xhow does X behave, looked at alone?P(X = 1) = p₁ — T₁'s true win rate
joint of (X, Y)how do they behave together?the 2×2 table: P(both win), P(only T₁), P(only T₂), P(neither)

The marginals do not determine the joint. Knowing p₁ = 0.55 and p₂ = 0.50 pins down the row and column totals of the 2×2 table — but many different tables have those same totals. Covariance lives in the joint: cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y], and E[XY] = P(both win) is a joint cell the marginals leave free.

2. The knob nobody tells you about

In a probability course the joint distribution is given and you compute from it. In simulation the situation is reversed: you write the program that generates the randomness, so you choose the joint. A choice of joint distribution with prescribed marginals is called a coupling of those marginals.

Two couplings of the same pair of Bernoulli marginals:

  • Independent coupling — draw U and V as separate uniforms; X = 1{U < p₁}, Y = 1{V < p₂}. Joint = product of marginals; cov = 0. This is what you get by default when each battle asks the RNG for fresh numbers.
  • Shared-draw coupling — draw one uniform U and reuse it: X = 1{U < p₁}, Y = 1{U < p₂}. Same marginals — U alone is still uniform from each side's point of view — but now the outcomes are chained: since p₂ < p₁, every T₂ win forces a T₁ win. The disagreement (X = 1, Y = 0) happens only when U lands in the sliver [p₂, p₁); the cell (X = 0, Y = 1) has probability exactly zero.

Under the shared draw, the per-pair difference X − Y is 1 on that sliver and 0 everywhere else: var(X − Y) = 0.05 · 0.95 ≈ 0.047. Independent, it's p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂) ≈ 0.497. Ten times less variance from the same two marginal coins — nothing changed except which joint you built. (Aside for the shape of the field: the coupling that maximises P(X = Y), which this shared-U trick achieves, is the classic optimal-coupling construction; CRN is that idea deployed as a variance tool. There is also an opposite knob — antithetic variates use U and 1−U to force covariance negative, which is what you want when summing instead of differencing.)

3. Why nothing biases

The worry: "if I rig the draws, don't I rig the answer?" No — and the reason is one line. Expectation is linear unconditionally: E[X − Y] = E[X] − E[Y] holds for any dependence whatsoever, because each expectation is computed from its own marginal, and the coupling left both marginals untouched. Averaged over many paired battles, the estimate of the gap p₁ − p₂ lands in the same place under any coupling. What the coupling moves is only the spread around that place — via the cov term in var(X − Y) = var X + var Y − 2 cov(X, Y). Coupling is a variance knob with the bias bolted at zero.

5. Hook back into CRN

Reread 0006 §1 with today's vocabulary and it collapses to three sentences. A battle simulator's randomness is an input you allocate, so estimating two win rates means choosing a coupling of the two estimates. Independent seeding chooses the product coupling (cov = 0); common random numbers choose a shared-draw coupling to force cov > 0, which the identity then subtracts from var(Δ̂). The paper's whole contribution (XDD) is about where in a trajectory the shared-draw choice is provably the right one.

6. Check yourself

A coupling changes…

Marginals are prescribed and preserved; the mean follows from the marginals by linearity (§3). The joint — how the pair co-moves — is the only thing being designed (§2).

The marginals survive the shared draw because…

U is a perfectly good uniform from either side's solo point of view — 1{U < p} is Bernoulli(p) regardless of who else reads U (§2). Options b and c are both false as stated.

With one shared U and thresholds 0.55 (T₁) and 0.50 (T₂), the pair (T₁ loses, T₂ wins) happens…

U < 0.50 implies U < 0.55: that joint cell is exactly zero (§2 — see it stay empty in Part B). Option b describes the opposite disagreement, (T₁ wins, T₂ loses).

Coupling leaves the estimated difference unbiased because…

E[X − Y] = E[X] − E[Y] needs no independence; each term reads only its own untouched marginal (§3). Variance is not linear (that's the whole identity), and covariance being nonzero is the goal, not a problem.
