The self-play loop, BR vs ABR, and Double Oracle

One loop, five symbols. The ORACLE is the step that makes the next battle policy; Double Oracle is what happens when that step is an exact best response.

Lesson 0002 · 2026-08-19 · ~12 minutes · glossary sheet · mission · serves the battle-policy experiment in Why Self-Play is consider Multi-Agent.md (the SP / FP / DO arms) · previous: 0001

Why this, now. Your battle-policy plan ranks three self-play arms — pure self-play, fictitious play, "double oracle" — in a cross-play matrix. The survey you are reading gives every such arm the same skeleton. After this lesson you should be able to say, in one breath, which box each arm sits in, why the "double oracle" arm is not actually Double Oracle, and why that matters for what you can claim. One vocabulary warning up front: the survey's ORACLE is not the matchup predictor. CONTEXT.md bans "oracle" as a name for the predictor; the survey's ORACLE is the battle-policy training step. Different slot, no conflict.

1. The loop — five symbols

The survey (🟢 Zhang, Xu et al., arXiv 2408.01072 v4, Sec. 3.1, Algorithm 1) says every self-play method is this, repeated for each epoch e and each policy k:

initialise π[k]h  →  π[k]h ← ORACLE(π[k]h, σ[k], Π)  →  𝒫 ← EVAL(Π)  →  Σ ← MSS(𝒫)

SymbolThe paper's sentenceYour word for it
Π"Each policy π[k] in the policy population Π … K denotes the policy population size."the pool of battle-policy checkpoints (plus the random and BC baselines) that enter the cross-play matrix
σ[k]"in our framework, σ[k] is the opponent sampling strategy" — a distribution over Πwhom the next checkpoint trains against
ORACLE"an abstract computational entity that returns a new policy adhering to specific criteria"the training step — PPO in every VGC-Bench arm
EVAL → 𝒫"performance matrix" — an Elo column, or the whole payoff matrixthe cross-play win-rate matrix; Glicko
MSS"takes the performance matrix 𝒫 as its input and produces a new interaction matrix Σ … If the MSS produces a constant matrix … the evaluation step can be skipped."the rule that tells the three arms apart

Read the arrows: ORACLE reads one row of Σ (who to train against) and writes a policy into Π; MSS closes the loop by turning the scoreboard 𝒫 into the next Σ. The survey's own summary of where the action is: "most previous work has concentrated on the ORACLE and MSS" (Sec. 3.6.3).

2. What the ORACLE returns — BR vs ABR

"(1) One type is the BR oracle, which is designed to identify the optimal counter-strategies against an opponent's strategy, including finding NE (McMahan et al. 2003). However, it often requires considerable computational effort. (2) To alleviate the computational demands, the approximate best response (ABR) oracle is introduced, using techniques such as RL (Algo. 2), evolution-theory-based methods (Algo. 3) or regret minimization methods (Algo. 4). (3) Other specially crafted ORACLEs are either tailored to specific meta-strategy solvers (MSSs) … or introduced to enhance diversity." 🟢 Survey, Sec. 3.1.

BR is the true arg-max: given the opponent mixture σ, the policy that maximises expected payoff against exactly σ. In a matrix game that is one column scan. In Pokémon it is a search over every possible policy — not computable. ABR aims at the same target but stops at "better than before": run RL against opponents sampled from σ (Algo. 2), or replicator dynamics, or regret matching. Lanctot et al. describe the swap in one line: "instead of computing exact best responses, we compute approximate best responses using reinforcement learning" (🟢 PSRO, arXiv 1711.00832, Sec. 2).

The trap: the guarantee lives in the oracle, not the MSS

Double Oracle's "converges to Nash" is proved from the oracle being exact ("Since ri is a best response we conclude that ∀p V(p, qi) ≥ vℓ" — 🟢 McMahan, Gordon & Blum 2003, Sec. 4.2). Keep the Nash MSS and swap in PPO and the theorem is gone; what is left is the PSRO heuristic. The survey files the result accordingly: "the oracle in DO is a BR rather than an ABR" (Sec. 3.3.2), and "PSRO (Lanctot et al. 2017) extends DO … the oracle is computed using RL techniques" (Sec. 3.3.3).

3. Watch Double Oracle run

The survey walks rock-paper-scissors through its framework (Sec. 3.3.2, Equ. 9). Here it is live. Payoff is to the row player: +1 win, −1 loss. Press DO step and read the log: each step is one MSS call (Nash of the shaded restricted game) followed by two ORACLE calls (exact best responses over all rows/columns).

  1. After iteration 1 the restricted game is just Rock vs Rock; Nash is trivially "Rock"; the best response is Paper. Compare the survey: "Assume that in the first iteration, the policy π1 is set to rock. Obviously, the NE-based MSS returns σ2 = (1,0,0). In the second iteration, the BR oracle adds paper."
  2. Watch the bounds vℓ ≤ value ≤ vu. They only meet when both oracles return something already in Π — that is the stopping rule, and Theorem 1 says the restricted-game Nash is then a Nash of the full game.
  3. RPS is the worst case Lanctot et al. name: "the entire strategy space may have to be enumerated … Rock-Paper-Scissors, whose only equilibrium has full support." Every strategy entered Π.

Now a four-strategy game. Same rock-paper-scissors cycle plus a fourth strategy that loses to all three by 0.6 — a badly piloted team, say. Start from Rock again.

The fourth strategy never enters Π: no best response ever points at it. That is the point of Double Oracle — McMahan et al.: "it is reasonable to expect in practice that many pure strategies will never enter R̄ and C̄." The MSS never has to price it, and the payoff matrix the MSS solves stays 3 × 3. In your setting the "strategies" are battle-policy checkpoints, so the analogue is: a checkpoint that nothing needs to beat never has to be sampled as an opponent.

5. Retrieval practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back. Options are equal length on purpose.

In the survey's loop, which component reads a row σ[k] of Σ and writes a policy into Π?

ORACLE(π[k]h, σ[k], Π) takes the opponent-sampling row and returns a new policy. EVAL reads Π and writes 𝒫; MSS reads 𝒫 and writes Σ.

Which oracle type does the survey assign to Double Oracle?

Sec. 3.3.2: "the oracle in DO is a BR rather than an ABR, computing the BR against the current NE opponent strategy of the restricted game."

Double Oracle stops when —

McMahan et al.: "If on some iteration i, ri is already in R̄ and ci ∈ C̄, then the algorithm terminates" — equivalently vℓ = vu. Growing to full size is the worst case, not the rule; full support is what RPS happens to need, not a criterion.

VGC-Bench's "double oracle" arm keeps DO's Nash MSS but trains with PPO. In the survey's taxonomy it is —

Survey Sec. 3.3.3: PSRO "extends DO … the oracle is computed using RL techniques"; Lanctot et al.: "Double Oracle is an instance of PSRO with n = 2 and σT set to a Nash equilibrium profile." Swap BR for ABR and it is PSRO-Nash.

What happens to DO's finite-iteration Nash guarantee when the oracle is PPO?

The proof uses "Since ri is a best response…" and a finite strategy set. PPO breaks both. Lanctot et al. state convergence for DO only and describe PSRO as "trading convergence precision". No source read gives a theorem for a PPO oracle.

Does CONTEXT.md's ban on "oracle" collide with the survey's ORACLE?

CONTEXT.md lists "oracle" under Avoid for the matchup predictor. The survey's ORACLE produces battle policies and never touches f. Write "the survey's ORACLE (= the battle-policy training step)" in the vault and nobody is confused.
